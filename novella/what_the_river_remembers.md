# What the River Remembers

## A Novella

---

*For those who loved across the wreckage of the world,*
*and for the children born of silence.*

## CHAPTER ONE

### The Net

The snow had been falling since before dawn, a slow and deliberate descent that turned the streets of Warsaw into something almost beautiful, almost clean. Helena Wojtera watched it from the window of the fourth-floor flat on ulica Mokotowska, her breath fogging the glass, her fingers curled around the edge of the curtain as though she might tear it away if she held on too tightly. Below, the street was empty save for a dog—some mangrel thing, brown and rib-thin—picking its way through the drifts with its nose pressed to the ground, searching for scraps that the snow had already buried.

She checked the clock on the wall. Seven minutes past six. Marysia would need to wake soon. The girl had been coughing in the night, a dry, rattling sound that had kept Helena awake until nearly three, lying on her pallet beside the child's bed, pressing her palm against Marysia's small back and feeling the heat radiate through the thin cotton of her nightgown. By morning the fever had broken, but Helena had not slept. She rarely did, not deeply, not anymore. Sleep in occupied Warsaw was a shallow country, a place you visited with one eye open and your ears tuned to the sound of boots on cobblestone.

She let the curtain fall and moved to the small stove in the corner of the room, crouching to feed it with the last of yesterday's coal. The fire caught reluctantly, spitting and smoking before settling into a sullen glow. The flat was cold. It had been cold since November, when the last proper delivery of fuel had come and the price had tripled anyway. Helena had stopped counting the zlotys. She counted calories now, and degrees, and the number of hours between one roundup and the next.

In the other room—really just an alcove separated by a threadbare curtain—Marysia stirred. Helena heard the small cough, the rustle of blankets, and then the girl's voice, still thick with sleep:

"Mama?"

"I'm here, kochanie." Helena crossed the room and drew the curtain aside. Marysia was sitting up in bed, her dark hair tangled, her eyes too bright and too large in her thin face. She was nine years old and she looked seven, or perhaps eleven—children in wartime aged in strange and uneven ways, their bodies growing slow while their eyes grew old. "How do you feel?"

"My throat hurts a little."

Helena pressed the back of her hand to the girl's forehead. The skin was warm but not feverish. "Drink." She held out the cup of water she'd left on the nightstand. Marysia drank obediently, then lay back against the pillow.

"Are you going to school today?"

Helena's chest tightened. School. The word had become a fiction they both maintained, a comfortable lie. There was no school for Polish children in the Generalgouvernement. Not really. There were secret lessons in cellars and back rooms, taught by teachers who risked imprisonment or death for the privilege of passing on the forbidden inheritance of their language and history. Marysia attended one such room, three streets over, run by a former professor named Pani Kowalska who had lost her husband in the September campaign and her two sons to camps she would not name aloud.

"Not today," Helena said. "You'll stay home."

Marysia's face fell. "But I feel better—"

"You stayed in bed all week with the cough. One more day." Helena smoothed the girl's hair. "Pani Kowalska will understand. Rest today, and tomorrow I'll take you myself."

The girl nodded, though Helena could see the disappointment. Marysia was at that age where she wanted desperately to be normal, to be a child among other children, to carry a satchel and complain about lessons and trade bread crusts for marbles. Helena had no way to give her this. She could not give her normal. She could give her only safety, and even that was a currency that devalued daily.

She returned to the kitchen alcove and set about preparing what passed for breakfast. Bread—dark, dense, barely risen—that she'd traded two hours of mending to obtain. A thin smear of margarine. A cup of barley water that she called tea. She ate standing, as she always did, one hand on the windowsill, watching the street below for any sign of movement that wasn't the wind or the thin dog.

The routine was essential. It was what kept them alive. Every morning, Helena performed the same ritual of observation: the street, the courtyard, the building across the way. She noted which windows were dark, which doors had new locks, which faces appeared at familiar hours. She had mapped the rhythms of the block—the old man who swept his stoop at seven-fifteen, the woman who hung laundry on Tuesdays regardless of weather, the young man from the second floor who hadn't been seen in three weeks and whose absence was a silence that spoke louder than any scream.

Today the street was quiet. This was unusual. On a Wednesday morning at this hour there were usually at least a few figures hurrying to the tram stop, heads bowed against the snow, shoulders hunched. But the street was empty. Helena pressed her face closer to the glass.

Then she heard it. Not the street, but the next street over—ulica Nowowiejska. A sound she knew too well. The clatter of truck engines. The sharp crack of orders. And underneath it, rising and falling like a thing alive: the sound of people screaming.

A łapanka. A roundup.

Her body moved before her mind could catch up. She was already crossing to the alcove, pulling back the curtain, her hands finding Marysia's arms and pulling her upright. The girl's eyes were wide with the terror that all Polish children learned before they learned to read.

"Mama—"

"Listen to me." Helena's voice was steady, the voice she used for the worst moments, the voice she had forged in the first terrible days of the occupation and had kept sharp ever since. "We need to go. Now. The back stairs. Do you understand?"

"The front—"

"No. The back. Get dressed. Everything you have. The coat. The scarf. Wrap it around your face."

Marysia was moving now, small hands fumbling with buttons, with the rough wool of her coat. Helena shoved her feet into her own boots, grabbed the bag she kept packed by the door—a bag that always contained forged papers, a change of clothes for each of them, a small sum of zlotys rolled into a tight cylinder, a silver locket that was the last thing of value she owned. She had prepared for this moment every day for four years. She had rehearsed it in her sleep.

But rehearsing and living were different countries, and the border between them was crossed in a single heartbeat.

They made it to the landing. The stairwell was dark—the electricity had been cut to the building two days ago—and Helena moved by feel, one hand on the banister, the other gripping Marysia's wrist. Down three flights. The back door opened into a narrow alley that ran between the building and the wall of the next courtyard. Helena pushed it open and stepped out into the cold.

The alley was clear. They moved quickly, heads down, boots crunching on the snow. Helena knew this route. She had walked it a hundred times in her mind, mapping each turn, each hiding place, each possible dead end. Left at the end of the alley. Through the gap in the wall that separated the two courtyards. Across the second courtyard, past the coal bins, to the front gate that opened onto—

She stopped.

Ulica Wilcza was not empty.

A truck sat idling in the middle of the street, its canvas-covered back open, soldiers standing in the bed with rifles held casually across their chests. On either side of the vehicle, a cordon of men in green-grey uniforms formed a tightening perimeter, sweeping civilians from doorways and dragging them from behind walls. A woman with a baby. An old man in slippers. Two boys no older than twelve. All of them being herded toward the truck like cattle.

And at the center of it, directing the operation with calm, precise gestures, was an officer.

Helena pulled Marysia behind the coal bins, her heart hammering. They were not yet seen. The cordon was still fifty meters away, tightening its arc. She could feel Marysia trembling against her legs, could hear the girl's breathing go thin and fast.

*Think. Think.*

The alley behind them. Could they go back? No—the truck was on the main street, but the side streets would have secondary cordons. This was a proper operation, a large-scale sweep of the district. She could see at least twenty soldiers, perhaps more. This was not a random terror action. This was methodical.

A sound broke through her calculations. Marysia coughed—a single, sharp, rattling cough that cut through the morning air like a bell.

Helena's blood turned to ice.

One of the soldiers turned. He looked directly at the coal bins. He said something to the officer.

The officer turned.

And Helena saw his face.

The world did not stop. It did not slow. The snow continued to fall, the truck continued to idle, the civilians continued to cry out. But inside Helena's skull, something collapsed—a wall she had spent ten years building, a dam she had constructed stone by stone from silence and determination and the sheer animal will to forget.

Because she knew that face.

She knew the line of that jaw, the particular angle of those cheekbones, the color of those eyes—a grey-blue that in the Munich sunlight had looked almost silver. She knew the way his dark blond hair fell just above his collar, the same way it had when he was twenty-three and leaning over a seminar table, arguing with her about Heine. She knew that face from a thousand mornings in a small flat on Amalienstrasse, from evenings in the Englischer Garten, from a night in September when he had knelt on a bridge over the Isar and held out a ring.

Jochen.

Jochen von Brandt.

He stood there in his greatcoat, his leather gloves, the Iron Cross at his throat, and he was looking directly at her. The recognition came across the distance like a physical thing—a blow to the chest that drove the breath from her lungs.

For a moment—one of those moments that contains within it the collapse and reconstruction of an entire universe—he simply stared. His mouth opened slightly. His hand, raised mid-gesture to one of his men, froze in the air.

Then he began to move toward her.

"Mama," Marysia whispered. "Mama, who—"

"Run."

Helena grabbed the girl's hand and pulled her away from the bins, away from the alley, away from the only escape route that had existed. She ran. Not because she had a plan, not because she could see a path to safety, but because the alternative was to stand still, and standing still meant letting him catch them, and letting him catch them meant—

She didn't know what it meant. That was the terror of it. She didn't know what he would do. She didn't know what he *was*.

They ran through the courtyard, through the gap in the wall, back into the first alley. Behind them, she heard the officer's voice—shouting now, not in the clipped tones of military command but in something rawer, something that sounded almost like a name.

"*Helena!*"

She stumbled. Marysia's hand jerked in hers. The girl was small and fast but her legs were short and the snow was deep and she was still recovering from illness and she was slowing Helena down and Helena was—

"Let go, Mama," Marysia gasped. "Let go and run faster—"

"No!" Helena's voice cracked like a whip. "No, we stay together. We *always* stay together."

They reached the back door of the building. Helena fumbled with the latch, got it open, pushed Marysia inside. The stairwell. Up. She could hear boots behind them—heavy, rhythmic, coming fast. She seized Marysia's hand again and they climbed, two flights, three, Helena's lungs burning, the girl sobbing with exertion.

Fourth floor. The door. Keys. Her hands were shaking so badly she dropped them once, and Marysia—blessed, brilliant child—snatched them from the floor and shoved them into the lock herself.

Inside. The bolt. The chain. Helena threw her back against the door as though her body alone could hold it.

Silence.

Then: footsteps on the stairs. Slow now. Deliberate.

They stopped outside the door.

A knock. Not the battering knock of a raid. A single, measured rap. As though this were a social call. As though this were a man calling on an old friend.

"Helena. Please. Open the door. I'm not going to hurt you."

His Polish was perfect. Of course it was. He had always been good with languages. It was one of the things—

She stopped the thought. She would not go there. Not now. Not ever.

She stood with her back against the door, her chest heaving, Marysia pressed against her side, the girl's face buried in her coat. She could feel Marysia's tears through the wool.

"Go away," Helena said. Her voice was a whisper, but it was steady.

"Helena, I—"

"Go away or I'll scream. I'll scream and the whole street will know a German officer is at a Polish woman's door, and you can explain that to whoever you need to explain it to."

Silence. A long, stretching silence. She could hear him breathing on the other side of the wood. She could hear the faint creak of leather as he shifted his weight.

Then: "The child. Is that—how old is she?"

Helena closed her eyes.

"She's nine."

Another silence.

"I need to know," he said, and his voice had changed—lower now, rougher, as though something in his throat had tightened. "Her father. Helena, who is—"

"You have no right. You lost every right the day you—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Go away."

More silence. Then footsteps, receding. Down the stairs. A door opening and closing below.

Helena slid down the wall until she was sitting on the floor, Marysia still clutched to her chest. She held the girl and she rocked her, back and forth, and she did not cry. She had made a vow ten years ago that she would not cry for Jochen von Brandt, and she would keep that vow until the day she died, even if keeping it burned her from the inside out like a coal swallowed whole.

"Mama?" Marysia's voice was small, muffled by the coat. "Who was that man?"

Helena pressed her lips together. The truth sat on her tongue, sharp and poisonous. *That man is your father. That man is the reason you exist. That man is the reason I lie awake at night counting the ways the world could end.*

"No one," she said. "No one you need to know."

Outside, the snow continued to fall, covering the city in white, covering everything in white, as though it might—might, if the world were merciful, which it was not—cover everything and make it new.

## CHAPTER TWO

### The University Garden

Munich, October 1930.

The city announced itself to Helena Wojtera through the window of a third-class train carriage, appearing first as a smear of light on the horizon—the pale gold of gas lamps reflected in low clouds—and then resolving, as the train slowed, into something vast and solid and terrifyingly real. She pressed her forehead against the cold glass and watched the suburbs unfold: factories with their chimneystacks breathing white plumes into the autumn sky, tenement blocks grey as the Bavarian stone they were built from, and then, suddenly, the domes and spires of the city center rising above the rooftops like the towers of a fairy tale drawn in ink.

She was twenty-one years old. She had a suitcase full of clothes, a letter of admission to the Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität, and exactly forty-seven Reichsmarks sewn into the lining of her coat. She spoke German well—her father had insisted on it, her father who believed that languages were armor and that a girl who could move between tongues could move between worlds—but she had never been beyond the borders of Poland. The longest journey she had taken before this was the train from Kraków to Warsaw, and that had been a matter of hours, not the overnight passage that had brought her here, through the flat lands of Silesia and across the frontier into a country that had, within her lifetime, attempted to erase hers.

She told herself this didn't matter. She told herself she was not Polish in the parochial sense—she was a citizen of the intellectual world, a subject of reason and letters and the republic of ideas. The university would be neutral territory. The professors would not care about borders. The books would not ask her passport.

She was naive. She knew this even then, even as she told herself otherwise. But naivety, in the young, is not ignorance—it is the courageous refusal to let knowledge curdle into despair.

The train deposited her at the Hauptbahnhof, and she stepped onto the platform into a world of noise and movement and the particular quality of Bavarian autumn light that made everything look gilded, even the soot-stained walls of the station. She stood with her suitcase and her coat and her forty-seven Reichsmarks and she felt, for the first time, the full weight of her solitude.

The room she had been allocated—through a letter from a Polish aid society that assisted students studying abroad—was in Schwabing, the artists' quarter, in a building on Amalienstrasse that leaned slightly to the left, as though it had spent too long listening to the wind. The room was small: a bed, a desk, a washstand, a window that looked out over a courtyard cluttered with bicycles and laundry lines. The wallpaper was peeling. The radiator hissed like a creature in pain. It was perfect.

Helena set her suitcase on the bed, removed her coat, and stood in the center of the room and breathed. She was here. She was actually here. In Munich. In Germany. At the university where Richard Wagner had once walked, where Max Planck held forth, where Rainer Maria Rilke had written letters that made her weep. She was here, and the world was wide, and she was a woman who could read in four languages and write in three, and she was going to study philosophy and literature and she was going to become someone.

The first week was a blur of registration and orientation and the particular humiliation of standing in a foreign language and not understanding every word. Her German was bookish, precise, learned from grammar texts and her father's careful instruction. The German spoken by the other students was something else entirely—a living, breathing thing that moved at speed, that swallowed its endings, that was peppered with Bavarian idioms she had never encountered. She sat in lectures and caught perhaps half of what was said. She took furious notes. She went back to her room each evening and re-read them and filled the gaps with a dictionary.

The other students, for the most part, ignored her. She was a curiosity—a Polish woman in a department that was ninety percent male, and a foreign one at that. The German students were polite but distant. They formed their own clusters, spoke their own shorthand, laughed at jokes she didn't understand. She ate alone in the student canteen, sitting at the edge of a long table, her eyes down, her book open, performing the universal pantomime of a person who does not wish to be disturbed, even though what she wished—what she ached for, with an ache that sat in her throat like a stone—was for someone to look up and see her and speak.

It happened on the eighth day.

The seminar was on nineteenth-century German poetry—Heine, Hölderlin, Kleist—taught by a professor named Albrecht whose lectures were legendary for their brilliance and their unpredictability. He did not read from notes. He paced. He quoted from memory, entire stanzas, his eyes closed, his hands moving as though he were conducting an invisible orchestra. He demanded that his students argue, challenge, contradict. "Philosophy," he told them on the first day, "is not a museum. It is a battlefield."

Helena had read all of Heine. Her father's library had a complete edition, bound in green leather, and she had spent the summer before her departure reading it cover to cover, marking passages in pencil, arguing with the margins. Heine was her poet—she felt him in her bones, in the particular ache of exile that ran through his verses like a current, in the way he could hold beauty and bitterness in the same hand and refuse to let either go.

That day, the seminar was on *Buch der Lieder*. Professor Albrecht was in rare form, moving through the poems with a kind of electric intensity, pausing at a particular stanza from *Lyrisches Intermezzo*—"In the lovely month of May, when all the buds were springing"—and asking the class to account for the tension between the poem's surface sweetness and its undertow of despair.

A voice answered. A man's voice, from the far side of the room. Helena looked up.

He was sitting near the window, turned slightly in his chair, one leg crossed over the other, a pencil held loosely between his fingers. He was tall, even sitting down—you could see it in the length of his torso, the breadth of his shoulders beneath his tweed jacket. His hair was dark blond, cut short but not severely, falling just above his collar in a way that suggested he ran his hands through it often. His face was angular—high cheekbones, a strong jaw—but his eyes were what arrested her. They were grey-blue, the color of winter sky over a lake, and they had a quality she would later learn to name: an intensity that was not aggression but attention, a way of looking at you as though you were the most interesting text he had ever encountered.

"The tension," he said, "is not between sweetness and despair. It is between memory and desire. The poem is not about what happened. It is about what the speaker wishes had happened. The May is not a real May. It is a May that exists only in the imagination, and the despair comes from knowing the difference."

Professor Albrecht's eyes lit. "And how do you know this, Herr—"

"Von Brandt. Jochen von Brandt."

"Herr von Brandt. How do you know this?"

"Because," the young man said, and now he turned his head and his eyes found Helena across the room—not her specifically, not yet, but the general direction of the foreign students, as though his argument needed to reach everyone—"because the poem is written in the past tense. Everything in it has already happened. But the speaker cannot stop reconstructing it. He cannot stop going back and remaking it. The buds are always springing. The nightingale is always singing. The beloved is always about to appear. That is not memory. That is obsession. That is a man standing in the wreckage of his own heart and refusing to leave the scene."

The room was quiet. Helena felt something shift in her chest—a click, like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there.

She did not speak that day. She sat and listened and took notes and tried not to stare. But when the seminar ended and the students rose and shuffled toward the door, she watched him—watched him pack his books with a leisurely precision, watched him nod to a friend, watched him walk out into the corridor with the particular gait of someone who occupies space without apology.

She told herself she was not interested. She told herself this was dangerous territory—a Polish woman and a German man, in a country whose relationship to hers was written in blood and treaty and centuries of grievance. She told herself she had come here to study, not to become entangled.

She told herself many things in those early days. She would come to regret all of them.

---

He found her three days later, in the university library.

She was in the reading room, surrounded by stacks of books on romantic aesthetics, making notes in a ledger she had purchased from a stationer on Ludwigstrasse. Her pen moved in the precise, slanted hand her father had drilled into her—letters that leaned forward as though they were always trying to reach the next word before the current one was finished.

"Excuse me."

She looked up. He was standing at the end of her table, holding a book against his chest, looking at her with those grey-blue eyes.

"You're the Polish girl. In Albrecht's seminar."

"I am." She kept her voice neutral. "And you are the one who gave the lecture on Heine."

He smiled. It was not a charming smile—not calculated or performed. It was sudden, almost startled, as though the smile had happened before he'd decided to allow it. "I didn't give a lecture. I gave an opinion."

"It sounded like a lecture."

"Professor Albrecht thought so too. He told me afterward that I should either write my thoughts down or stop talking so much that I'm forced to have them."

Helena almost laughed. She caught it, held it back, but she felt it move through her—a warm, involuntary thing, like sunlight through a window she hadn't realized was open.

"Did you agree?" he asked. "With what I said? About the Heine?"

She considered him. The question was genuine—she could see that. He was not performing. He was asking because he actually wanted to know.

"I thought you were right," she said. "About the obsession. About the past tense. But I would go further. I would say that the poem is not about a lost love. It is about a lost country. Heine was writing from exile—from Paris. The May he remembers is not just a personal May. It is a German May. A Germany that still existed for him in the imagination, even as the real Germany was closing its doors to him because he was Jewish."

Jochen set his book down on the table. He pulled out a chair and sat. He did not ask permission, but the gesture was not presumptuous—it was the gesture of a man who had found a conversation he was unwilling to leave unfinished.

"That's a political reading," he said. "Albrecht would say the poem transcends politics."

"Albrecht has never been the subject of a poem."

Now he laughed—a real laugh, full and surprised, and several heads turned in the reading room and he lowered his voice. "You're not afraid of the professor, are you?"

"I'm afraid of many things. But a man who has spent his career inside a text is not one of them. What are you afraid of, Herr von Brandt?"

His smile faded slightly. His eyes—those eyes—held hers. "I haven't decided yet," he said. "But I suspect I'll find out soon enough."

They talked for two hours. They talked about Heine and Hölderlin and the nature of lyric consciousness. They talked about Schopenhauer, whom he admired, and Nietzsche, whom she loved, and the particular agony of reading Dostoevsky in the original Russian when your Russian was adequate but not excellent. They talked about the city—his ease in it, her strangeness within it—and about the political weather that was beginning to move through Munich like a storm front gathering on the horizon.

"You know," he said at one point, leaning back in his chair, "I've never met anyone who reads Heine the way you do. Most people here—they read it as nostalgia. As sentimentality. You read it as an act of resistance."

"That's what it is."

"Even when the poem was written before the resistance was necessary?"

"Poems are always written before the resistance is necessary. That's why they survive."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, quietly: "I'd like to show you something. The English Garden. It's beautiful this time of year—the leaves are turning. There's a Chinese tower where they serve beer, but we can walk past it and sit by the Kleinhesseloher lake. It's quiet there."

Helena hesitated. Every instinct told her to decline. She was a foreign woman in a foreign city, alone, with a man whose country had helped partition hers. She should be cautious. She should be careful. She should be—

"I'd like that," she said.

They walked through the garden in the October afternoon, the trees blazing with color, the light falling in long golden shafts through the canopy. The lake was as he had promised—quiet, mirror-still, reflecting the sky. They sat on a bench and watched the leaves fall and talked about everything and nothing, and at some point—she could never afterwards identify the exact moment—the distance between them on the bench ceased to exist, and his hand was covering hers, and she was not pulling away.

"What is your name?" he asked. "Your real name. Not the way they pronounce it in the seminar—like it's something to be careful with."

"Helena. My name is Helena."

"Helena." He said it slowly, tasting the syllables. "Helena Wojtera. The most beautiful foreigner in the most beautiful city in the world."

"You're drunk on beer and rhetoric."

"I'm drunk on you." He said it simply, without embarrassment, as though stating a fact he had only just discovered and was delighted to report. "I think I have been since you told Albrecht that a poem is always written before the resistance is necessary."

She should have been alarmed. She knew, even then, that this man was dangerous—not dangerous to her safety, but dangerous to the careful architecture she had built around her life: the walls of intellect, the fortifications of reason, the moat of distance she kept between herself and the world. He was going to breach those walls. She could feel it. She could feel the siege beginning.

She leaned into him. His arm came around her shoulders. The leaves fell. The lake reflected the sky.

And in Munich, in the autumn of 1930, with the storm gathering on the horizon and the brownshirts marching in the streets and the future pressing against the present like a hand against a window, Helena Wojtera fell in love.

---

The weeks that followed were the brightest of her life. She knew this even as she lived them—which is either a blessing or a curse, she would later think, depending on the hour and the bottle. She knew that what she had with Jochen was rare, was dangerous, was the kind of thing that the world did not generally permit to people like them—a Polish woman and a German man, in a city that was becoming the capital of a movement that viewed her kind as subhuman. She knew all of this and she went forward anyway, because love, in its early and terrible fullness, is not a rational enterprise. It is a surrender. It is the white flag raised above the citadel of the self.

They met every day. In the library. In the garden. In the small cafés of Schwabing, where they sat over coffee and cigarette and argued about everything—about philosophy and politics and the nature of beauty and the proper length of a line of poetry. He introduced her to his friends—a small circle of fellow students, mostly from the humanities, mostly liberal, mostly baffled by the political extremism that was growing around them like a weed. They accepted her with a warmth that surprised her, that made her feel, for the first time since arriving in Munich, that she might actually belong.

Jochen's family lived in Bavaria—his father was a minor industrialist, his mother a woman of impeccable social standing—and he spoke of them with an affection that was complicated by a clear-eyed awareness of their limitations. "My father is a good man who holds bad ideas," he told her one evening, as they walked along the Isar. "He believes in strength. In order. In the restoration of German pride. I understand why. The war took everything from his generation—my grandfather died at the Somme, my father came home with nothing, and then the hyperinflation, and the humiliation of Versailles. I understand his anger. I don't share it."

"Does he vote for them? The National Socialists?"

Jochen was quiet for a moment. "He sympathizes. He thinks they're too crude. But he thinks they're right about some things."

"And your mother?"

"My mother thinks whatever my father thinks, with a five-year delay."

Helena laughed. Then she stopped. "Your father's sympathy. Does it extend to people like me? Poles?"

Jochen's jaw tightened. "You are not *people like you*. You are Helena. You are the woman I—" He stopped. Took a breath. "Yes. He would have prejudices. He is a man of his time and his class. But he is not a monster. And when he meets you—when he *sees* you—he will understand."

"You think."

"I know."

She wanted to believe him. God, she wanted to believe him. But something in her—the Polish part, the part that had grown up on stories of partitions and uprisings and the slow grinding of a nation under foreign boots—would not let her. That part of her kept a register, a ledger of slights and warnings, a ledger that no amount of Bavarian charm could balance.

But she believed him about the other things. She believed him when he said he loved her. She believed him when he said the world they were building together—the world of books and ideas and long walks and shared beds in a tiny flat they found together on Amalienstrasse, a flat so small the bed was in the corner of the room where she studied—was real. Was permanent. Was not subject to the whims of politicians and the march of history.

She was wrong about that. But she was young, and she was in love, and being wrong about the permanence of love is perhaps the most human error there is.

They were happy. Fiercely, recklessly, achingly happy. They made love in the small flat with the ferocity of people who know—on some level, in some part of themselves they refuse to acknowledge—that the happiness is borrowed time. They argued about everything except the things that mattered, which they avoided with the careful choreography of two people who are afraid of what they'll find if they start looking. They cooked together—badly, because neither of them could cook—and ate at the desk, surrounded by books, and talked late into the night.

One evening in December, as the first snow of the season began to fall, Jochen took her to the Maximiliansbrücke. The Isar ran black beneath them, and the bridge lamps cast golden circles on the stone. He was quiet all the way there, and she could feel something building in him—a tension, a purpose. She knew what was coming before he spoke.

"Helena." He stopped in the middle of the bridge. He turned to face her. His breath came white in the cold air. "I don't want to go any more days without asking you this. I don't want to waste another day of whatever time we have."

He reached into his coat pocket. His hands—she noticed—were shaking.

"Marry me."

The ring was simple. Gold. No stone. He had made it himself, he told her later, in a workshop he'd found through a friend of a friend, because he wanted it to be something he had shaped with his own hands, something that was purely his and not his family's money or his family's name.

She said yes before he finished the question. She said yes three times, as though the word needed to be spoken multiple times to become real. She said yes and she was crying and he was crying and they were standing on a bridge over a black river in a city that was sleepwalking toward catastrophe, and it was the most perfect moment of her life.

She would carry that moment with her for the rest of her days. She would carry it into the occupation, into the roundups, into the nights when the sky over Warsaw glowed with the light of burning buildings. She would carry it like a coal in her chest, and it would keep her warm, and it would burn her, and it would never go out.

---

They told no one for three weeks. It was their secret—their small, bright, desperate secret. They carried it between them like a lit candle, shielding it from the wind. They made plans in whispers: a small wedding in the spring, after the semester ended. They would go to Kraków, she said, and he would meet her father, and her father would grill him for three hours about Kant and he would pass the test because he had read Kant in the original and her father would have to respect that. He laughed and said yes, and then he said he was terrified, and she said good, because her father was terrifying, and they laughed and held each other and for those three weeks the world was small and warm and safe.

Then, on a Tuesday in January 1933, Jochen came to the flat with a newspaper in his hand and his face the color of ash.

"Hitler has been appointed Chancellor," he said.

Helena took the paper. She read the headline. She read it again.

"When?"

"Today. Hindenburg appointed him today."

She sat down on the edge of the bed. The room seemed to tilt.

"Jochen. Jochen, do you understand what this means?"

"I understand." He was standing in the doorway, the newspaper hanging from his hand. He looked like a man who had been struck and was still trying to determine where the blow had landed. "I understand that the world I thought I was living in has changed, possibly irrevocably, in the last four hours."

"Your father. Your father voted for them, didn't he?"

Jochen did not answer immediately. When he did, his voice was very quiet. "He wrote to me this morning. He's... elated. He says this is the beginning of Germany's restoration. He says the von Brandt family should declare for the party openly."

"And you?"

"And I said I needed to think."

"Jochen." She stood. She crossed to him. She took his face in her hands. "You are not going to declare for the party."

"No." His voice was firm. "No, of course not. I'm not—" But there was something in his voice. A hesitation. A tremor. And Helena, who knew him so well by now that she could read the silences between his words like text, heard it.

"You're afraid."

"I'm afraid of my family. I'm afraid of what happens if I go against them. I'm afraid of what happens if I don't."

"What happens if you don't go against them, Jochen? What happens if you stand by while they—" She stopped. She could not finish the sentence. She could not say what she was afraid of, because saying it would make it real, and she was not ready for it to be real.

"They're not what you think," he said. "The party. The rhetoric. It's political theater. It'll moderate once they're in power. Once the responsibility of governance—"

"Jochen. Jochen, listen to me." She gripped his arms. "I grew up under occupation. Not this occupation, not the German one, but occupation nonetheless. My country was partitioned for a hundred and twenty-three years. I know what occupiers look like. I know what they say before they act and I know what they say after. The rhetoric is not theater. The rhetoric is the blueprint."

He pulled away from her. Not violently, but with a movement that was, in itself, a kind of violence—a withdrawal, a closing of a door. "You're being dramatic," he said.

"I'm being Polish."

"That's not—" He ran his hand through his hair. "Helena, I love you. You know I love you. But you have to understand my position. My family. My father. I can't just—you can't ask me to—"

"I'm not asking you to do anything except think. Except see what is happening. Except refuse to let your family—"

"My family is not the enemy!"

The words hung in the air between them. He looked as shocked as she felt. He had never raised his voice at her. Not once in the six months they had been together.

"Your family," she said, slowly and precisely, "is about to declare for a movement that considers people like me to be less than human. That is not an opinion, Jochen. That is a policy. That is in the book. Have you read *Mein Kampf*?"

"I've read excerpts."

"Read the whole thing. Read it and tell me it's theater."

He didn't answer. He left the flat without another word.

He did not come back that night.

He came back the next morning, unshaven, his eyes red, and he sat on the bed and took her hands and said: "I read it. All of it. Last night. I couldn't stop."

"And?"

"And I'm scared."

"Me too."

"I don't know what to do, Helena. My father has already signed up. My uncle. My cousin. If I refuse—if I speak against—I'm cut off. I lose everything. The money for the university. The apartment. Everything."

"And if you don't refuse? If you go along?"

He was silent.

"Jochen. If you go along with them, you lose me."

"I know."

"Then choose."

He looked at her. His eyes were those grey-blue winter lakes, and in them she saw something she had never seen before: the look of a man who knows that every available choice leads somewhere he does not want to go.

"I need time," he said.

"How much time?"

"I don't know. A week. A month. I need—"

"Time is what they want. Time is what they use. Every day you wait is a day they get stronger. Every day you wait, the people like me—" She stopped. She could not finish.

They sat like that for a long time, holding hands, the silence between them filled with everything they could not say.

She gave him two weeks.

It was not enough.

On the last day of those two weeks, Jochen came to the flat and he did not need to speak. She saw it in his face. He had chosen. He had chosen his family. He had chosen the von Brandt name and the money and the security and the terrible, seductive promise of belonging to the winning side. He had not declared for the party—not yet—but he had not refused, and in the mathematics of 1933, not refusing was the same as declaring.

She did not scream. She did not cry. She felt something inside her—a structure, a foundation, the thing she had been building since the day she arrived in Munich—crack and shift and settle into a new, harder configuration.

"Get out," she said.

"Helena—"

"Get out of my flat. Get out of my life. And if you ever come near me again, I will make you regret it."

He stood in the doorway and looked at her, and she could see the love still there—the real, terrible, useless love—and she could see the other thing, the thing that was larger than love: fear. The fear of a man who has been told his entire life that his worth is inherited, that his place in the world is a gift from his blood and his name, and who cannot bear to forfeit that gift, even for the woman he loves.

He left. She locked the door. She sat on the bed and she was very still for a long time.

Then she began to pack.

## CHAPTER THREE

### The Turning

What Helena had not told Jochen—what she had kept from him with a secrecy that surprised even herself—was that she had already met his family. Once. Without his knowledge.

It had happened in November, on a day when Jochen was away in Augsburg visiting a professor. His mother had written to him—a letter he had shown Helena, full of warm invitations and maternal enthusiasm—and Helena, in a moment of reckless curiosity that she would spend the rest of her life analyzing, had taken the train to the von Brandt estate in Starnberg without telling him. She had written to Frau von Brandt separately, identifying herself as a fellow student and friend of her son's, and had been received with genuine hospitality.

The house was beautiful. A villa in the Bavarian style—white walls, dark timber, geraniums in the window boxes—with a garden that sloped down to the lake. Frau von Brandt was a handsome woman of sixty, elegantly dressed, her grey hair arranged in a precise coiffure. She was gracious, curious, clearly intelligent. Over tea in the sitting room, she asked Helena about her studies, about Poland, about her family. Helena answered carefully, offering just enough to be interesting without revealing the depth of her investment in her son's life.

Then Herr von Brandt came in.

He was a big man—broad, solid, with Jochen's jaw and none of his son's gentleness. His handshake was firm to the point of pain. His eyes, when they landed on Helena, performed a rapid calculation that she recognized immediately: *foreign, female, poor*. The calculation was not hostile. It was the assessment of a man who categorized people by their utility and their threat.

"So you're the Polish girl," he said. "Jochen's mentioned you."

"He has?" Jochen had mentioned no such thing.

"Frequently. You're making quite an impression." He poured himself a brandy, though it was barely three in the afternoon. "What does your father do, Fräulein Wojtera?"

"He's a professor. Of literature. At the University of Warsaw."

A flicker of something—disdain? relief?—crossed his face. A professor was acceptable. A professor was not a threat. "Academics," he said. "Noble pursuit. Not particularly profitable, but noble."

"Mama," her husband called to the other room, "bring the good biscuits for Fräulein Wojtera. She's traveled a long way."

Frau von Brandt brought the biscuits and retreated. The conversation continued—surface things, weather, the university, the political situation. And then Herr von Brandt set down his brandy and leaned forward, and the mask of courtesy shifted, just slightly, to reveal what was underneath.

"You know, Fräulein Wojtera, I'm glad Jochen has a Polish friend. It's good for young people to have international perspectives. But I hope you understand that we Germans have certain... responsibilities. Certain duties to our own people. The world has been very unkind to Germany. The war, the reparations, the Diktat of Versailles. A nation cannot endure such treatment without eventually demanding justice."

"I understand," Helena said. "Poland has had similar experiences."

"Indeed." His eyes narrowed slightly. "Though Poland's situation is rather different, isn't it? Your country is... new. Fragile. Built on territory that rightfully belonged to others."

The room went cold. Not literally—the fire was burning, the tea was hot—but something in Helena's blood temperature dropped, a physiological response to the recognition of a predator's logic. She knew this argument. She had heard it in Polish newspapers, in German newspapers, in the mouths of men who spoke of the Polish Corridor as though it were a wound in Germany's side rather than a scar on Poland's body.

"The territory belonged to Poland for centuries before the partitions," she said, keeping her voice level. "The borders were drawn by three empires that decided Poland did not need to exist. That is not fragility, Herr von Brandt. That is survival."

He studied her. Then he smiled—a smile that was not warm. "You have spirit. I like that. But spirit doesn't change history. History is written by the strong, and the strong, Fräulein Wojtera, are reasserting themselves. That is what Herr Hitler represents. Not cruelty. Not barbarism. *Strength*. The restoration of what was taken."

"Even if what was taken belonged to someone else?"

His smile sharpened. "Belonged. An interesting word. Who decides what belongs to whom?"

"The people who have lived there for a thousand years."

"Sentimental." He drank his brandy. "But I appreciate the sentiment. It's very Polish."

He did not say *Jew*. He did not need to. The word was in the room, unspoken, present in the way that a body is present in a room after it has left—a shape in the air, a negative space. Helena was Polish, which in the von Brandt calculus was already a category below German, but Polish was survivable. Polish could be accommodated, at least in polite company, at least when the Polish woman was sitting in your parlor and your son seemed to care about her.

It was the rest of the calculus that frightened her. The calculus that extended beyond her to the millions of others who did not have the protection of a university degree and a German-speaking boyfriend. The calculus that would, if Herr von Brandt's logic were carried to its conclusion, reduce entire populations to raw material for the strong.

She left the villa an hour later. She took the train back to Munich. She did not tell Jochen she had been there. She said nothing about the conversation. But something had shifted in her—a plate in the tectonic architecture of her hope had moved, and the tremor, though small, was the first signal of what was to come.

---

The weeks between that visit and Hitler's appointment as Chancellor were a period of accelerating dread. Helena watched the streets of Munich change. She watched the brownshirts grow bolder, their marches longer, their slogans sharper. She watched theSA men set up checkpoints in working-class neighborhoods and heard, secondhand, about the beatings, the arrests, the first tentative acts of state-sanctioned violence. She watched the university change—the way certain professors suddenly adopted a harder tone, the way Jewish students found themselves excluded from study groups, the way the word *völkisch* began to appear in conversations that had previously been about aesthetics and metaphysics.

And she watched Jochen change.

It was subtle at first. A hesitation before he answered her questions about his family. A new defensiveness when she raised politics. A tendency to change the subject when the radio brought news of Nazi atrocities. He was not becoming a Nazi—he was doing something more complicated and, in some ways, more terrible. He was accommodating. He was making the small compromises that large tyrannies require: looking away, changing the subject, telling himself that the worst would not come, that the system would moderate itself, that the violence was the work of a few extremists and not the logical expression of an ideology.

She confronted him. Once. Twice. Three times. Each time, the conversation followed the same pattern: she presented evidence, he acknowledged it, he explained it away, she pushed harder, he retreated into silence, and then—crucially—he did not change. Not in any way that mattered. He did not speak out. He did not join any opposition group. He did not refuse the party membership card his father sent him. He simply continued to exist in the space he had always occupied, a space that was becoming smaller and more compromised by the day.

"You're asking me to be a hero," he told her on the night before the Chancellor appointment. They were in the flat, the radio playing softly in the background. "You're asking me to break with my family, to lose my financial support, to risk my education, my career—everything—for what? A principle? Principles don't pay rent, Helena."

"No. But they keep you human."

"Do they? I've seen a lot of principled people. Your professor friends. The socialists. They're all going to be in camps if the Nazis—"

"So you're going to accommodate them? To stay safe?"

"I'm going to *survive*. There's a difference between accommodation and endorsement."

"Is there? When the accommodation enables the endorsement?"

He couldn't answer that. He never could. That was the thing about Jochen von Brandt that she loved and that would eventually destroy her: he was not a coward. He was not cruel. He was not stupid. He was simply unwilling to pay the price that conviction demanded, and he was clever enough to construct elaborate justifications for that unwillingness that sounded, to anyone who wanted to hear them, like reasonableness.

The morning after Hitler's appointment, the streets of Munich were full of celebration. Swastika flags appeared in windows that had been bare the day before. SA men marched in triumph. Strangers embraced in the squares. It was, by all accounts, a festival—and Helena walked through it like a ghost, like a woman moving through the dream of a madman, watching the celebration with the detached clarity of someone who already knows the ending.

She went to the university. The campus was divided—some students celebrating, others standing in shocked silence, a few openly weeping. She found her way to her seminar room and sat at her desk and opened her notebook and stared at the blank page and could not write.

Jochen came to the flat that evening. He was pale. He had not shaved. He sat on the bed and said: "My father called. He wants me to come home this weekend. He says the family needs to present a united front. He says—"

"He says you need to declare."

"He says it would be good for my future. If I join now, while the movement is still growing, I'll have a position when—"

"Stop."

He stopped.

"Jochen. I need you to hear me. Truly hear me. If you join that party, if you declare for that movement, we are finished. I will not marry a man who wears a swastika. I will not bring children into a world where their father has allied himself with people who want to erase people like me. I will not live in a country that is doing this. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then what is your answer?"

Silence. A long, terrible silence.

"I can't leave my family," he said.

"And I can't stay."

The words were out of her mouth before she had decided to speak them. She heard them as though from a distance. She felt the floor shift beneath her. This was the moment. This was the door, opening, and on the other side of it was a life she had not planned, a life without Jochen, without Munich, without the future they had built.

"Helena—"

"I'm leaving, Jochen. I'm going home. To Poland."

"You can't. The university—"

"I don't care about the university. I care about what the university is becoming. I care about what *you* are becoming."

"I'm not becoming anything. I'm staying the same. I'm still—"

"You're staying the same. That's the problem. You're going to stay the same while the world changes around you, and you're going to call it pragmatism and you're going to believe it because the alternative is too costly. And that is the most dangerous thing a man can do—to know what is right and to choose what is easy and to call the choice rational."

He stood. He crossed to her. He knelt beside the chair where she sat and he took her hands and he looked up at her with those grey-blue eyes, and she could see the love there—still there, still real, still the most genuine thing about him—but she could also see the other thing, the fear, the calculation, the willingness to let the world burn as long as his own corner of it remained warm.

"Don't go," he said. "Please. Stay. We can still—"

"Still what? Be engaged? Be married? Raise children in a country where half the population considers them defective? Where your father considers *me* defective? No, Jochen. That is not still. That is surrender. And I will not surrender."

She stood. She removed her hands from his. She walked to the window and looked out at the street below, where a group of young men were marching past with a swastika banner, their faces bright with the intoxication of belonging, of being on the right side of history for the first time. She watched them pass, and she did not turn around, and she heard Jochen's breathing behind her—heavy, ragged, the breathing of a man who is losing something he cannot replace.

He left at midnight. She heard his footsteps on the stairs. She heard the front door close. She stood at the window for a long time after that, watching the street empty, watching the last of the marchers disappear around the corner, watching the city she had loved turn, brick by brick, into something she did not recognize.

The next morning, she went to the university administration and withdrew her enrollment. She went to the train station and bought a ticket to the border. She went back to the flat on Amalienstrasse and packed her suitcase—the same suitcase she had arrived with eight months earlier, now lighter, because some of the things she had brought with her had been left behind in the city, in the library, in the garden, in the bed, in the arms of a man who had chosen his blood over his heart.

She left the ring on the nightstand. She could not bring herself to wear it, and she could not bring herself to throw it away. She left it where he would find it, and she wrote a note: *I return what you gave me. I cannot keep a promise made to a man who no longer exists. Do not look for me. Helena.*

She took the train. She crossed the border. She entered Poland on a grey morning in February 1933, with a suitcase, forty-two Reichsmarks, and a child she had not yet conceived growing somewhere in the future like a seed in winter earth.

---

She did not discover the pregnancy until April.

She was back in Warsaw, living in her parents' flat on ulica Marszałkowska. Her father asked no questions about why she had returned early from Munich. Her mother asked once, received a look, and did not ask again. Helena enrolled at the University of Warsaw. She found work translating German documents for a publishing house. She began the slow, deliberate process of rebuilding a life that did not include Jochen von Brandt.

The sickness came first as a suspicion. She was tired—tired in a way that sleep did not cure, tired in a way that felt cellular, as though her body were doing work she had not assigned it. Her breasts were tender. Her stomach moved strangely in the mornings. She counted the days and realized, with a jolt that felt like falling, that she had not bled in seven weeks.

She went to a doctor. A woman—a sharp-eyed, no-nonsense gynecologist named Dr. Zielińska who had delivered more babies than she could count and had developed, as a result, a complete immunity to surprise.

"You're pregnant," she said, after the examination. "Approximately eight weeks. Is this what you expected?"

"No."

"Is the father—" She paused. "Does he know?"

"He knows I exist. That is all."

Dr. Zielińska studied her. "You're not married."

"No."

"In Poland, an unmarried mother—"

"I know what an unmarried mother is in Poland."

The doctor nodded. She was not judging. She was assessing. "What do you want to do?"

Helena sat on the examination table, her legs dangling, her hands in her lap. She was twenty-two years old. She was carrying the child of a German man who had chosen his family's allegiance to a Nazi movement over his love for her. She was in a country where an illegitimate child was a social death sentence, where a Polish woman who bore a German man's child would be—what? A traitor? A whore? A woman who had lain with the enemy?

She thought of Jochen. She thought of his face, his hands, the way he had looked at her on the bridge. She thought of the ring on the nightstand. She thought of his father's smile. She thought of *Mein Kampf*, which she had read cover to cover in the weeks since leaving Munich, and which she now understood was not a political manifesto but a blueprint.

"I want to keep it," she said.

Dr. Zielińska raised an eyebrow. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

"The father—"

"The father does not exist."

Another long look. Then the doctor nodded. "Then we proceed. You'll need prenatal care. I'll refer you to a colleague. And you'll need to decide, before the child is born, what you're going to tell people."

"I'm not going to tell people anything. The child will have my name. The father will be listed as unknown. That is the end of it."

"And if people ask?"

"Then they will learn that some questions do not require answers."

Dr. Zielińska smiled—the first time since Helena had walked in. "You're going to be a difficult woman," she said. "I think that's exactly what you'll need to be."

---

She told her parents in June, when the pregnancy was visible and could no longer be hidden by loose clothing. The conversation took place in her father's study—a room lined with books, smelling of pipe tobacco and old paper, the room where she had first discovered Heine and had fallen in love with the idea of a world larger than the one she'd been born into.

Her father listened to her announcement without speaking. He sat in his leather chair, his pipe unlit in his hand, his face an unreadable mask. Her mother sat beside him, her hand on his arm, her eyes wide.

"Who?" was all her father said.

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters to me."

"It doesn't matter because he's gone. He's not coming back. He's not involved. The child is mine and only mine."

"Helena." Her father's voice was very quiet, which meant it was very dangerous. "You are carrying a child out of wedlock. In this city. In this country. In this *family*. Do you understand what this means?"

"I understand perfectly."

"Your mother's family. My colleagues. The university. People will talk."

"Let them talk."

"They will say you're a whore."

"Then they will be wrong, and I will not care."

Her mother began to cry. It was a quiet crying—tears without sound, the kind of crying that comes from a place deeper than shock, from the place where a mother's vision of her daughter's future meets the reality of what that future has become.

"Please, Helenko," her mother said. "Please tell us who. If he hurt you—if there was something—"

"No one hurt me. I chose. I chose and I accept the consequences."

Her father set his pipe down. He stood. He crossed to the window and looked out at the street for a long time. Then he turned back to her, and his face was not angry—it was something worse. It was sad. It was the face of a man who is watching his daughter walk into a fire and knows he cannot stop her.

"The child will carry your name," he said. "And my name, if you'll allow it. The father will not be recorded. That is what you want?"

"That is what I want."

"And you will not contact this man. This German. You will never contact him."

"I've already left him."

"Swear it."

"I swear it."

He nodded. Once. As though the nod settled something in him—a decision, a resignation, a letting-go. "Then it is decided," he said. "You will stay here. You will have the child. And we will manage."

They managed. Her mother found a doctor who would ask no questions. Her father told his colleagues that his daughter had had a brief and unfortunate romance and that the subject was closed. The household absorbed the pregnancy the way a household absorbs a death: quietly, completely, with the understanding that grief—because that is what it was, a grief for the life Helena might have had—would be carried in silence and would not be discussed.

On a cold morning in December, in a small private hospital on the outskirts of Warsaw, Helena gave birth to a girl. The doctor placed her on Helena's chest—slick, red, squalling, perfect—and Helena looked down at her and felt something crack open inside her, something vast and terrifying and unconditional.

"Marysia," she whispered. She had chosen the name months ago, lying awake at night, her hand on her belly, talking to the child she could not yet see. Małgorzata. Marysia. A name that was Polish, that was clean, that belonged to no man. "Marysia."

The baby blinked. Her eyes—newborn eyes, not yet focused, not yet the color they would become—stared up at Helena with an expression that was not love, not yet, but was something like trust. A raw, unformed trust. The trust of a creature that has just arrived in a world it does not understand and has decided, on the basis of almost no evidence, to believe that it will be safe.

Helena held her daughter and made a vow. She would never contact Jochen von Brandt. She would never reveal to anyone—the child, her parents, the world—that Marysia's father was a German who had chosen his family's Nazi allegiance over his love for her. She would raise this child as a Pole, as her own, as a child who had no father because no father was better than a father who wore a swastika.

She would keep this vow. She would keep it through the invasion, through the occupation, through the ghetto, through the uprising, through the years of hunger and fear and loss. She would keep it even when keeping it became the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the birth, harder than the flight from Munich, harder than the night she heard the first bombs fall on Warsaw.

She would keep it because Marysia deserved a mother, not a history. She deserved a life, not a legacy. She deserved to be a child, not a symbol of a love that had been destroyed by the world's indifference to the hearts of people.

And she would keep it until the day a German officer in Warsaw looked at her through the snow and she understood that vows, like walls, are only as strong as the world's willingness to respect them. And the world, in 1943, had very little willingness left.

## CHAPTER FOUR

### The Reckoning

They did not leave the flat for three days.

Helena bolted the door and drew the curtains and sat with Marysia on the narrow bed and held her and did not sleep. The girl had stopped crying after the first hour—she had learned, in her nine years of life, that crying was a luxury that the world did not generally permit—but she trembled continuously, a fine, low vibration that Helena could feel through the layers of clothing, through skin and bone, as though Marysia's body were trying to shake itself free of the memory.

"What did that man want, Mama?" she asked on the second day, her voice small and flat, the voice she used when she was trying not to be afraid.

"He wanted nothing. He was lost. He knocked on the wrong door."

"But he said your name."

Helena closed her eyes. Marysia was nine. She was not a fool. She had heard the officer call out *Helena*—not *Frau*, not *you*, but her name, spoken with a familiarity that contradicted the lie.

"He knew me. From a long time ago. Before the war. Before you." She chose her words with the care of a surgeon choosing instruments. "It doesn't matter. He's gone."

"Is he coming back?"

"No."

The word was a prayer. She spoke it into the dark space between the bed and the wall, into the smell of coal dust and old plaster and the particular odor of a room that has been lived in too long without ventilation. *No. He is not coming back. He is a ghost. He is a memory that has learned to walk and wear a uniform, but he is not real. He cannot hurt us. He cannot—*

But she did not believe it. She had seen his face. She had seen the recognition, the shock, the something else—the thing behind the shock that she refused to name. Jochen von Brandt had not become simpler in the intervening decade. He had become more complicated. And a complicated man who wore a German officer's uniform in occupied Warsaw was not a man who could be dismissed with a prayer.

On the third day, the food ran out.

Helena had nothing. She had traded her last marketable possession—a wool shawl that had been her mother's—for bread two days before the roundup, and the bread was gone, and the jar of preserves was empty, and the barley water was finished. She listened to Marysia's stomach growl and felt a rage so pure and so hot that it was almost indistinguishable from love.

She had to go out. She had no choice.

She waited until midday—the hour when the patrols were thinnest, when the streets were most likely to be populated by civilians rather than soldiers, when a woman walking quickly and purposefully might pass without drawing attention. She dressed Marysia in every layer they had, wrapped the scarf around the girl's face, and opened the door.

The stairwell was empty. The street, when she emerged from the building's front entrance, was cautiously inhabited—a few figures moving with the careful, non-committal gait of occupied people: heads down, eyes forward, hands visible. Helena took Marysia's hand and they walked.

The market—what remained of it—was three streets over, in a square that had once been a pleasant open space with benches and a fountain and had become, under occupation, a place of transaction and desperation. The stalls were fewer now. The prices were obscene. The bread was adulterated with sawdust and the potatoes were small and green and the meat—if you could call it meat—was of uncertain origin. Helena had fourteen zlotys. It was enough for two days of bread and perhaps a small bag of potatoes if she haggled hard enough.

She bought the bread. She bought the potatoes. She was turning to go when she saw the notice.

It was posted on the wall of the square, a large official placard with the German eagle and the swastika and text in both German and Polish. A public announcement. Helena's German was excellent—she had been educated in it, after all—and she read it without meaning to, automatically, the way one reads a street sign.

*BEKANNTMACHUNG. All civilians detained in the roundup of 25 February in the Mokotow district are to be processed at the Sammellager on ulica Szucha. Families with children under the age of twelve may apply for release through the local Ortsschutz command. Applications must be submitted in person by a parent or legal guardian. Deadline: 28 February.*

Helena read it twice. She read it a third time. Then she took Marysia's hand and she walked home, and she did not breathe until they were behind the bolted door of the flat, and she sat on the bed and stared at the wall and felt the world tilt on its axis again.

The Sammellager. The collection point. Marysia was in the system. Her name—her false name, the name on the forged papers, Krystyna Nowak—was on a list in a German office. If Helena did not apply for her release, Marysia would remain in the camp. If Helena did apply, she would have to go to the Ortschutz command on ulica Szucha—the headquarters of the Gestapo in Warsaw—and she would have to stand before German officers and claim a child and explain why that child had been caught in a roundup and produced papers that were, if examined closely, forgeries.

It was a trap. Every part of it was a trap. The notice was too convenient, too specific, too *helpful*. In Helena's experience, the Germans did not help. They lured. They opened a door and waited to see who would walk through it, and then they closed the door behind them.

But Marysia was nine years old and she was in a camp. Or she was supposed to be in a camp. The roundup trucks had been heading to Szucha. The children had been separated from the adults. Marysia was—somewhere—in the system.

Helena held her daughter and counted the hours.

---

Jochen von Brandt stood on the stairwell landing and listened to the bolt slide home and the chain engage and the silence that followed—the silence of a door that has been closed not merely against a visitor but against an entire history.

His hands were shaking. He looked at them as though they belonged to someone else—these hands in their leather gloves, these hands that had spent the morning directing a roundup, that had signed processing forms, that had ordered the loading of civilians into trucks with the same casual efficiency with which he had once, in another life, turned the pages of a poetry book.

*Helena.*

He said the name in his mind and it filled the stairwell like smoke. He had not spoken her name in ten years. He had not permitted himself to think it. He had built, in the architecture of his own mind, a sealed room where she lived—a room he never entered, never opened, never acknowledged. And now she had been standing in front of him, in a street in occupied Warsaw, holding a child, and the room had blown open and everything that had been sealed was loose and moving through him like shrapnel.

He walked down the stairs. He walked out of the building. He stood in the snow and looked up at the fourth-floor window. The curtains were drawn. He could not see in. He could not see her.

The child.

He had seen the child—a girl, small, dark-haired, frightened. Nine years old. He had done the mathematics before the thought could fully form, because mathematics was what he did now, mathematics was his profession: the timeline of Helena's departure, the months that followed, the pregnancy she had hidden, the birth. Nine years. It fit. It fit with a precision that was either coincidence or destiny, and Jochen von Brandt was not a man who believed in destiny, which meant he was a man who was, at this moment, desperately trying not to believe in destiny.

She had not told him. He understood this with a clarity that was almost physical. She had carried his child across a border, into a country his nation would invade, and she had raised that child alone, without a word to him, without a single letter or message or sign. She had chosen silence over contact. She had chosen obscurity over the von Brandt name. She had chosen—

*She had chosen well,* a voice in his head said. *She had chosen better than you chose.*

He walked back to his billet. He was quartered in a requisitioned apartment on Aleja Szucha—appropriately, given that the Gestapo headquarters was at the end of the same street. The apartment had once belonged to a Polish lawyer; it still had his books, his photographs, his life pressed into the margins of the rooms like fingerprints. Jochen had made no effort to remove these traces. He lived among another man's belongings like a man who knows he does not deserve his own.

He sat at the desk. He removed his gloves. He placed his cap on the table. He sat with his hands flat on the wood and he thought.

He had not become a Nazi. This was the thing he told himself, the thing he had told himself for ten years, the thing that was the load-bearing wall of his self-regard. He had not joined the party. He had not worn the uniform of the SA or the SS. He had not attended the rallies, had not sung the songs, had not raised his arm in the salute. He had, technically, remained unaffiliated.

But he was here. He was in Warsaw, in 1943, wearing the uniform of the Wehrmacht, carrying the authority of the occupying power, directing roundups that sent civilians—men, women, children—to camps and processing centers and, from there, to places he tried not to think about. He was, in every way that mattered, a cog in the machine. He had not joined the party, but he had joined the army. He had not declared for Hitler, but he had declared for Germany, and in 1943, the distinction was academic.

He had told himself, for years, that he was serving his country, not the regime. He had told himself that the army was different from the party, that the Wehrmacht was an institution of honor, that his role was administrative, that he had never fired a weapon at a civilian, that he had—on several occasions—intervened to prevent excesses. He had told himself these things, and they were, in a narrow and technical sense, true.

But they were also, in a larger and more important sense, the most elaborate lie he had ever told.

He was here because he had not been willing to leave. He had not been willing to pay the price that Helena had asked him to pay. He had chosen his family, his name, his position, his safety—and now those choices had led him to this: a stairwell in Warsaw, listening to a door close, knowing that the woman he had loved was on the other side of it with a child that might be his, and that he had no right—no right at all—to either of them.

He sat in the darkening room. He did not turn on the light. He did not eat. He sat and he thought about Helena, and about the child, and about the ten years that had passed between them like a wound that had healed over something still infected.

Then he stood. He went to the filing cabinet. He found the roundup manifests. He found the list of detainees from the Mokotow operation. He read through the names, searching.

*Nowak, Krystyna. Age 9. Female. Status: Pending processing.*

There she was. Marysia—Krystyna, on paper—was in the system. And if the processing continued according to the standard timeline, she would be moved within forty-eight hours to the transit camp at Pruszkow, and from there the children would be distributed to various fates: some to Germanization programs, some to labor camps, some to the ever-expanding machinery of disappearance that consumed the occupied territories like a fire that could not be fed.

Jochen closed the file. He sat back. He looked at the ceiling.

He could get her out. He had the authority. A signature, a stamp, an annotation in the margin—*release, error, administrative correction*—and the child would be free. It would take five minutes. It would cost him nothing.

Except it would cost him everything. Because getting the child out would mean seeing Helena again. It would mean going back to that door. It would mean entering the sealed room and finding, inside it, everything he had spent ten years trying to forget.

He signed the release.

The knock came on the morning of the fourth day.

Helena had decided. She would go to Szucha. She would apply for Marysia's release. She would walk into the mouth of the beast and she would take her child back. She had spent the night composing her cover story in German, rehearsing it, testing it for weaknesses, hardening it like steel in a forge. She would be a Polish widow—her husband had died in the September campaign—searching for her daughter. She would produce the papers. She would be calm, respectful, unremarkable. She would be invisible.

The knock interrupted her preparation. Three sharp raps—military precision. Her stomach dropped.

She went to the door. She did not open it.

"Frau Wojtera." The voice came through the wood, muffled but recognizable. "Please. I'm alone. I have something for you."

She pressed her ear to the door. She could hear him breathing. She could hear the faint creak of leather. She could hear the building around them—the radiator hissing, a door closing somewhere below, the muffled sound of a radio playing music from the other side of a wall.

"Slide it under the door," she said.

"I can't. It's not paper."

Silence.

"Helena. The child. Krystyna. She's in the Sammellager on Szucha. I've signed her release. But she needs to be collected in person. You need to come to the processing office. I'll be there. I'll make sure—"

"How do I know this isn't a trap?"

"You don't." His voice was flat, stripped of everything but fact. "You don't know. You have to trust me. Or you don't have to trust me, and you go to Szucha yourself and you take your chances with whoever is sitting at that desk. But I'm telling you—she's been processed as Nowak. She'll be moved to Pruszkow in two days if she isn't collected. And once she's in Pruszkow—"

He stopped. He didn't need to finish. They both knew what happened in Pruszkow. They both knew what happened to unclaimed children in the transit camps. Some of them were Germanized—sent to families in the Reich, their Polish names erased, their identities overwritten. Some of them disappeared entirely. The machinery of the occupation did not distinguish between children and adults when it came to the business of consumption.

"Why?" Helena's voice was barely audible. "Why are you doing this?"

A long pause.

"Because I'm a coward, Helena. I've been a coward for ten years. This is the least I can do."

Another silence. Helena closed her eyes. She could feel Marysia behind her, in the alcove, watching her with those too-old eyes. She could feel the weight of every choice she had ever made pressing down on her like weather.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "I'll be there in twenty minutes."

"No." His voice sharpened. "Not you. Send someone else. An old woman. A neighbor. Someone who won't—"

"Who won't what? Who won't recognize you? Who won't ask why a German officer is personally ensuring the release of a Polish child?"

"Helena—"

"Twenty minutes. Ulica Szucha. The processing office. If you're not there, if this is anything other than what you say it is, I will find a way to kill you that will be slow and that you will remember for the rest of your extremely shortened life."

She heard him breathe. Then, very quietly, he laughed. It was the same laugh he had given in the library in Munich, ten years and a war ago—surprised, full, helpless.

"Twenty minutes," he said.

The footsteps receded. Down the stairs. Out of the building. Gone.

Helena turned from the door. Marysia was standing in the alcove, fully dressed, her coat buttoned, her scarf wrapped around her face. She was holding the bag—the bag Helena kept packed by the door.

"I packed," she said.

Helena's throat closed. She crossed the room and knelt in front of her daughter and took the bag from her hands and set it aside and held the girl's face in her palms.

"You're going to Pani Kowalska," she said. "She's going to keep you until I come back. Do you understand?"

"Are you going to get me out of the camp?"

"Yes."

"Are you going to see that man again?"

Helena hesitated. Marysia's eyes were dark and steady. The girl was nine, but she was not a child—not in the way that children in peacetime were children. She was a creature of the occupation, shaped by curfews and roundups and the constant, low-frequency terror of a world in which adults could disappear without warning. She understood, in a way that no nine-year-old should understand, that the world was not safe, that her mother was the only thing between her and the dark, and that anything—*anything*—that threatened that protection was a thing to be watched.

"Yes," Helena said. "I'm going to see him. But I'm bringing you home. Do you trust me?"

Marysia considered this. Then she nodded, once, with the solemn gravity of a person making a decision.

"I trust you, Mama."

Helena kissed her forehead. She stood. She took the girl's hand. And together they walked out of the flat and down the stairs and into the snow-covered street, toward a destiny that neither of them could name but both of them could feel, pressing against the present like a hand against glass.

## CHAPTER FIVE

### The Processing Office

Ulica Szucha was a street that Helena had avoided for four years. It was the spine of the German administrative district in Warsaw—the street along which the institutions of occupation arranged themselves like predators along a riverbank. The Gestapo headquarters occupied the corner building, a hulking structure of pre-war Polish modernism that had been converted, with characteristic German efficiency, into a place of interrogation and fear. The windows were blacked out. The entrance was guarded. The cars that came and went were dark-green and unmarked, and the people who were brought out of them were not the same people who had been put in.

Helena had heard the stories. Everyone in Warsaw had heard the stories. The cells in the basement where the interrogators worked with rubber truncheons and electrified wire. The holding rooms where prisoners waited for days without food or water, listening to the sounds from the other cells. The courtyard where, on certain mornings, the bodies were laid out for identification—bodies that had been alive the previous evening, bodies that had been people with names and families and plans for the following week.

She walked down this street now, holding her daughter's hand, and the cold entered her not through the skin but through something deeper—a cold that was not meteorological but existential, the cold of a woman who knows that she is walking toward the place where her worst fears live.

Marysia's hand was tight in hers. The girl was not speaking. She was walking with the careful, measured gait of a child who has learned that drawing attention is dangerous, that the correct response to fear is stillness, that the body must not betray what the mind is feeling. Helena envied her this discipline. She herself was trembling.

The processing office was in a building halfway down the street—not the Gestapo headquarters itself, but an annex, a place where the bureaucratic business of occupation was conducted: the registration of civilians, the processing of detainees, the administration of labor assignments and resettlement orders. It was, in its way, more terrifying than the headquarters, because it was ordinary. It had a waiting room. It had a counter. It had forms and stamps and the mundane apparatus of administration. It was the banality of it that made it horrible—the way it transformed atrocity into paperwork, the way it made the destruction of human lives a matter of filing and routing and cross-referencing, as though the people who passed through its doors were not people at all but entries in a ledger.

Helena pushed open the door. The waiting room was half-full—women, mostly, with children, all of them quiet, all of them staring at the floor. A German clerk sat behind a high counter, writing in a ledger. He looked up when they entered. His eyes performed the same calculation as Herr von Brandt's had, ten years ago: *female, child, Polish. Category: irrelevant.*

"Number?" he said, in German.

"I was told to come for a release." Helena's German was flawless. The clerk's eyebrows rose slightly—a Polish woman speaking German without accent was unexpected. "For the child detained in the Mokotow roundup. Krystyna Nowak."

He checked his ledger. His finger traced a line. "Nowak. Krystyna. Yes. She's been released. The authorization came from—" He paused. His eyebrows rose higher. "From Oberleutnant von Brandt."

Helena's face did not change. She had expected this. She had prepared for this. But hearing the name spoken in this room, in this context, by this clerk who regarded it as nothing more than an administrative fact—this was a different thing from hearing it whispered through a door.

"My office is down the hall. Third door on the right." The clerk's voice was neutral, bored, the voice of a man who processes hundreds of releases a week and for whom this one is no different from any other. "The Oberleutnant said he would meet you there."

Helena nodded. She took Marysia's hand. They walked down the hall.

The corridor was long and white and brightly lit—the kind of lighting that left no shadows, no corners, no place for secrets. The walls were bare except for a portrait of Hitler that hung at the far end, the Führer's eyes following them as they walked with the painted intensity of a man who believes, with absolute sincerity, that the world exists for his rearrangement.

The third door on the right was open. Jochen stood behind a desk, his hands resting on its surface, his uniform pressed, his cap on the chair beside him. He looked, in this light, older than he had in the street. The lines around his eyes were deeper. His face had lost the softness that remained in Helena's memory—the last traces of the young man who had argued about Heine in a sunlit seminar room. This was a man shaped by ten years of compromise, his face a landscape of small surrenders.

Marysia pressed against Helena's leg. Helena put her arm around the girl.

"Thank you for coming," Jochen said. His voice was controlled, careful, the voice of a man speaking in a room where the walls had ears. "Please. Sit."

Helena did not sit. She stood in the center of the room, holding Marysia, and she looked at Jochen with an expression that was not anger—anger would have been simpler, easier, more manageable. What she felt was something closer to grief: the grief of a woman looking at the ruins of the person she had loved and recognizing, in the rubble, the original architecture.

"Where is she?" Helena said.

"She's in the holding room down the hall. I've already processed the release. She just needs to be—" He glanced at Marysia. His face changed. Something moved behind his eyes—a recognition that went deeper than the physical, a recognition that bypassed the mind and went straight to some older, more primal part of him. He looked at the girl—this small, dark-haired, frightened child—and his mouth opened slightly, and his hand came up as though to reach for something, and then he stopped himself.

"She looks like you," he said. Very quietly. Almost involuntarily.

Helena's arm tightened around Marysia. "She looks like her father."

The words hung in the air between them. Jochen's face went white. He looked from Helena to Marysia and back again, and Helena watched the mathematics happening behind his eyes—the confirmation, the collapse, the sudden and total restructuring of everything he had thought he knew about the last ten years.

"You didn't tell me," he said.

"No."

"How long—"

"Ten years. She's nine. You can count."

His hand gripped the edge of the desk. His knuckles went pale. For a moment—just a moment—Helena saw the mask slip. She saw the man underneath—the man who had held her on a bridge over the Isar, the man who had knelt in the snow and offered her a ring he had made with his own hands. She saw the love there, undiminished, as though ten years and a war and a child had been nothing more than a long afternoon, and the bridge was still there, and the ring was still in his pocket, and the world had not yet learned how to destroy things.

Then the mask came back. The officer reasserted himself. The jaw set. The eyes narrowed. The voice, when it came again, was measured and calm and utterly controlled.

"I want to help. Both of you. I can—"

"You want to help." Helena's voice was cold. It was the coldest she had ever been in her life—colder than the morning she had left Munich, colder than the night she had heard the bombs. "You want to help. You directed the roundup that caught my daughter. You stood in the street and you sent children into trucks. And now you want to *help*."

"Helena—"

"What would you have done, Jochen? If you hadn't recognized me. If I had been any other woman, with any other child. Would you have signed the release? Would you have noticed? Would you have cared?"

He didn't answer. He couldn't. The answer was no. They both knew it. He had signed the release because the child was his. If the child had been anyone else's—if Helena had been anyone else's—she and Marysia would be in the system right now, waiting for processing, waiting for Pruszkow, waiting for whatever fate the machinery of occupation had in store for unremarkable Polish civilians with forged papers and no connections.

"Exactly," Helena said. "That's what I thought."

"Mama," Marysia whispered. The girl was looking at Jochen with an expression of such concentrated attention that Helena was startled—this was not the look of a frightened child. This was the look of a child who was *studying* someone, who was reading a face the way she had been taught to read a text, with care and precision and the understanding that what she found here would matter.

"Marysia." Helena crouched to her level. "Go down the hall. The clerk will take you to the holding room. You'll see the other children. I need to speak to this man alone."

"Mama—"

"Go. I'll be right behind you."

Marysia hesitated. Then she moved toward the door. At the threshold, she stopped and looked back at Jochen. The look she gave him was not hostile. It was not warm. It was the look of a child assessing a variable—adding a new data point to a calculation she was too young to complete but old enough to begin.

Then she was gone.

The room was silent. The two of them stood on opposite sides of the desk, the portrait of Hitler watching from the wall, the snow falling outside the window.

"You should leave Warsaw," Jochen said. "Both of you. Leave. Go to the countryside. Somewhere the roundups don't reach. I can arrange papers—"

"You can arrange papers." Helena laughed. It was a terrible sound—dry, sharp, stripped of humor. "The forger who made my daughter's papers was good enough to get her through four years of occupation. What more could you possibly offer?"

"Better papers. Safer papers. Helena, the situation in Warsaw is deteriorating. The roundups are getting larger. The ghettos are being liquidated. The entire city is—"

"I know what the city is. I live in it. You visit."

He flinched. The word landed like a blow. *You visit.* As though the occupation were a business trip. As though the suffering were something he observed rather than something he administered.

"I didn't choose this," he said. "You know I didn't—"

"You chose everything. That's the point, Jochen. That's the entire point. You chose your family. You chose your name. You chose your safety. And every choice you made led you here—to this room, to this desk, to this uniform. You did not fall into this life. You built it. Brick by brick. Choice by choice."

"I was twenty-three."

"I was twenty-one. And I left."

The silence that followed was absolute. Jochen looked at her, and she looked at him, and between them lay ten years of unspoken things—the letters he had never sent, the ring she had left on the nightstand, the child who was walking down a corridor in a German administrative building, carrying the blood of both of them in her veins like a secret encoded in her cells.

"I think about her," he said. "Every day. I think about you. Every day. That's why I—" He gestured, vaguely, at the room, the desk, the uniform. "That's why I signed the paper. Because for ten years I have done nothing—*nothing*—that was for you. Not one thing. And today, for the first time, I did something that mattered to someone other than myself. And I would do it again. Whatever the cost."

Helena closed her eyes. She felt something shift inside her—not forgiveness, not yet, not anything so clean or so simple. But a crack in the wall. A fissure in the architecture of her rage. She had spent ten years building this wall, and it was strong, and it was necessary, and it was the thing that had kept her alive. But walls, she had learned, do not only keep things out. They keep things in. And the things she had kept inside—the love, the grief, the fury, the longing—had been growing in the dark for a decade, and they were, now, very large.

"I need to go to my daughter," she said.

"Helena. Wait." He came around the desk. He stopped an arm's length away. "The papers I'm going to give you. They're not just travel documents. They're labor assignment exemptions. They'll protect you from the roundups. They'll protect the child. I'm going to register you both as essential workers—translator, seamstress, whatever your cover is. It will keep the Gestapo off your street."

"Why are you doing this?"

"Because I can. Because it costs me nothing. Because the child—" His voice broke. Just slightly. Just for a moment. "Because the child is—"

"Don't say it."

"—mine."

The word fell into the room like a stone into water. Helena felt the ripples move through her—the shock of hearing it spoken aloud, the first time, after ten years of silence. *Mine.* Not *yours*. Not *ours*. *Mine.* As though the claiming were a right he had earned by standing in this room and signing a paper, as though fatherhood were a bureaucratic category that could be conferred by an act of administration.

"She doesn't know," Helena said. "She doesn't know who you are. She doesn't know you exist. And that is how it will remain."

"Helena—"

"She will grow up Polish. She will grow up free—or as free as anyone can be in this country. She will not grow up carrying the name of a man who—"

"Who what? Who serves his country? Who does his duty? Who follows orders?" His voice had risen. The control was slipping. "Is that what you think I am? A monster? A willing participant in—"

"I think you're a man who knew what was right and chose what was easy. And I think the child deserves better than to carry that knowledge in her name."

They stared at each other. The room was very quiet. Outside, the snow was falling harder—the kind of heavy, deliberate snowfall that transformed landscapes, that buried boundaries, that made the world look, for a while, like something new.

"Take the papers," Jochen said, finally. His voice was low, drained. "Take them and go. I'll have them ready by tomorrow. Come back to the flat on Aleja Szucha—my billet. I'll leave them in the mailbox by the entrance."

"I'm not coming to your flat."

"Then I'll leave them somewhere else. A dead drop. A café. Name the place."

Helena considered this. She was exhausted. She was angry. She was carrying inside her a weight of emotion that she did not have the strength to sort through—not now, not in this room, not under the painted eyes of a man who was planning the destruction of her country. But Marysia was waiting. Marysia was in a holding room down the hall, and she was nine years old, and she had been through enough for one day.

"The café on the corner of Nowy Swiat and Aleje Jerozolimskie. Tomorrow. Noon. If you're not there in ten minutes, I'm gone, and you'll never see either of us again."

"I'll be there."

She turned. She walked to the door. At the threshold, she stopped. She did not turn around.

"For the record," she said. "I did not come here for the papers. I came here for my daughter. If you had not been here—if some other officer had been processing her release—I would have taken her and left and I would never have looked back. You are not the reason I survived, Jochen. Do not pretend that you are."

She walked out. Down the hall. To the holding room where Marysia was waiting. She opened the door, and her daughter looked up at her with those dark eyes—the eyes that were hers, the eyes that were also his, the eyes that held in them the genetic argument of two people who had loved each other across the wreckage of the world—and Helena knelt and gathered the child in her arms and held her and held her and did not let go.

## CHAPTER SIX

### The Space Between

The walk home was a dream—a walking dream in which the streets were familiar and alien at the same time, in which the snow fell and the city breathed and Helena moved through it with her daughter's hand in hers like a woman moving through a landscape she had seen only in paintings. Marysia was quiet. She had not spoken since leaving the holding room. She walked with the careful, deliberate gait of a child who has witnessed something she does not yet understand and is trying, with all the resources of her nine-year-old mind, to file it away in the correct category.

They reached the building. They climbed the stairs. They entered the flat. Helena locked the door and bolted it and drew the curtains and sat on the bed with Marysia in her lap and breathed.

"Mama."

"Yes, kochanie."

"That man. He's my father, isn't he?"

The world stopped. Helena held very still. She had prepared for this question—she had prepared for it a thousand times, rehearsing answers, discarding them, rehearsing again—and now that it had come, now that it was here in the room with them, sitting on the narrow bed like a third presence, she found that none of her prepared answers were adequate. None of them were true.

"Yes," she said.

Marysia was quiet for a long time. She was looking at her hands—small hands, chapped from the cold, the nails bitten short. When she spoke again, her voice was steady.

"Is he a bad man?"

Helena closed her eyes. This was the question. This was the one she had feared more than the roundup, more than the Gestapo, more than the camps. This was the question that had no good answer. If she said *yes*, she was telling her daughter that half of her blood was bad, that the source of her existence was a thing to be ashamed of, that the man who had released her from a camp that morning was a monster. If she said *no*, she was betraying everything she had built, everything she had sacrificed, every vow she had made.

"He was my husband, once. Before the war. Before you. We loved each other very much. Then the war came, and his family chose a side, and he went with them. And I chose a different side. And I came home. And I had you. And you are the best thing I have ever done."

"Is he coming back?"

"I don't know."

"Do you want him to?"

Helena looked at her daughter. Marysia's eyes were dark and clear and utterly without judgment. She was asking a question, not making an accusation. She was nine years old, and she was trying to understand the world, and she was using the only tool she had for that purpose: the truth, as she understood it.

"I don't know that either," Helena said.

Marysia nodded, as though this were a satisfactory answer. Then she leaned her head against Helena's chest and closed her eyes, and within minutes she was asleep—the deep, exhausted sleep of a child whose body has finally decided that it is safe enough to stop being afraid.

Helena held her. She held her for a long time. And in the quiet of the flat, with the snow falling outside and the radiator hissing and the child sleeping in her arms, Helena Wojtera wept for the first time in ten years.

---

The resistance cell met that evening in the basement of a pharmacy on ulica Zielna. There were five of them: Helena; Marek, a former engineer who had become the cell's leader; Zofia, a young woman who ran messages between cells and whose face was so unremarkable that she could walk past a Gestapo patrol without being noticed; Tomasz, a teacher who forged documents; and Pani Irena, a widow of fifty who had lost three sons to the war and had decided, with the clear-eyed fury of a woman who has nothing left to lose, to make the Germans pay for every one.

Helena was late. She was never late. This was the first thing Marek noticed.

"The roundup," he said, when she slipped through the door. "We heard. Were you caught?"

"No. My daughter was."

The room went still. Zofia's hand went to her mouth. Pani Irena closed her eyes.

"She's home now," Helena said. "An officer signed her release. She's safe."

"An officer." Marek's voice was careful. "Which officer? Why would a German officer—"

"He's someone I knew. Before the war."

The silence that followed was different from the first silence. The first had been shock. This one was suspicion. Marek was looking at her with the expression of a man trying to solve an equation with insufficient variables. Zofia was looking at the floor. Tomasz was looking at Helena's hands.

"How did he know where to find you?" Marek asked.

"He saw me. In the street. During the roundup."

"And he recognized you."

"Yes."

"And instead of arresting you, he released your daughter."

"Yes."

"And then?"

"And then nothing. He gave me papers. That's all."

Marek leaned back in his chair. He was a tall man, thin, with the gaunt face of someone who had not eaten properly since 1939. His eyes were his most notable feature—dark, intelligent, perpetually assessing. He had been a resistance fighter since the first week of the occupation, and his assessment of people was as precise as his engineering calculations.

"Helena," he said, very quietly. "You understand what this looks like. A German officer who personally ensures the release of your child. Who provides you with papers. Who *knows where you live*. This is not how the occupation works. The Germans do not help people. They use people. If this man is helping you, it is because he wants something from you. And what he wants will cost us more than it costs him."

"I know."

"Do you? Because from where I'm sitting, it looks like you're in a position where a German officer has leverage over you. And leverage, in this war, is the most dangerous currency there is."

"He wants nothing from me."

"Everyone wants something."

Helena looked at him. She could not tell him the truth. She could not tell any of them. The knowledge that Marysia's father was a German officer would not merely compromise her position in the cell—it would destroy it. In the underground, collaboration with the enemy was punishable by death. And the line between collaboration and survival was so thin, so gossamer, so easily crossed, that even the people who walked it could not always tell which side they were on.

"I'll be careful," she said.

"See that you are." Marek's eyes held hers for a moment longer, then shifted to the table. "We have work. The transport schedules for next week. Tomasz has the new forgeries. We need to move them to the collection point on Praga by Friday."

The meeting continued. Helena performed her role—the distribution of tasks, the assessment of risks, the careful coordination of movements that would, if successful, save lives and, if discovered, end them. She was good at this work. She had been good at it for four years. It was the work that had kept her sane during the darkest periods of the occupation—the work that gave her suffering a purpose, that transformed her grief into action, that made her a participant in the fight rather than merely a victim of the war.

But tonight, the work felt different. Tonight, with the knowledge of Jochen sitting inside her like a stone in her stomach, everything felt compromised. Every plan, every risk, every calculation was shadowed by the awareness that she was, in some small and secret way, indebted to the enemy. That the papers in her coat pocket—the papers that would protect her and Marysia from the next roundup—had been signed by a German officer's hand. That the life she was fighting to preserve had, in one particular and crucial instance, been preserved by the very system she was fighting to destroy.

She left the meeting with the taste of ash in her mouth.

---

The café on the corner of Nowy Swiat and Aleje Jerozolimskie was called *Pod Zygmuntem*—a small, dark place with wooden tables and a counter where they served coffee that tasted of chicory and bread that tasted of sawdust and the desperate hope that it would be enough to keep you alive until tomorrow. It was, like every café in Warsaw, a place where people performed normalcy—a place where you could sit and read a newspaper and drink a cup of something warm and pretend, for the length of a cup, that the world outside the window was not on fire.

Helena arrived at five minutes to noon. She chose a table in the corner, her back to the wall, the door in her line of sight. She ordered coffee. She did not drink it. She watched the door.

Jochen arrived at noon precisely. He was in civilian clothes—a dark overcoat, a grey scarf, a felt hat pulled low. Without the uniform, he looked younger. He looked, for a moment, like the man he had been in Munich—the student, the poet, the young man who had argued about Heine and made love in a small flat and knelt on a bridge. The illusion lasted exactly three seconds before his eyes found hers across the room and the look he gave her—a look of such concentrated, desperate attention that it was almost physical—shattered the illusion and replaced it with something more complicated and more true.

He sat across from her. They regarded each other. The waitress came. He ordered coffee. She did not drink hers either.

"The papers." Helena held out her hand.

He reached into his coat and produced a manila envelope. He placed it on the table. She opened it. Inside: two sets of identity documents, two work permits, two Kennkarten—registration cards that identified the bearers as essential civilian workers exempt from roundup and deportation. The papers were flawless. The names were different from her own—she would be *Anna Kowalczyk*, Marysia would be *Ewa Kowalczyk*—but the photographs were good, the stamps were authentic, the bureaucratic apparatus of identity was complete and convincing.

"These are good," she said.

"They'll hold up to anything short of a full forensic examination. Which the Gestapo doesn't generally perform on civilian workers. They're too busy with more important things."

"Like what?"

"Like finding the people who are killing their soldiers."

Silence. The reference to the resistance hung between them like a wire. Helena's hand tightened on the envelope.

"I'm not going to ask you about your work," Jochen said, quickly. "I don't want to know. I'm telling you this so you understand what the papers protect you from. The Gestapo is increasing its operations. The roundups are going to get worse. Spring will bring—" He stopped. He looked at his hands. "I shouldn't be telling you this."

"Why are you?"

"Because I can't—" His voice dropped. "I can't protect you any other way. I can't be seen with you. I can't come to your flat. I can't—Helena, do you understand what would happen to me if anyone knew? About the child. About us. About—"

"I understand perfectly."

"Do you? Because I think you think I'm here because I want to be. I'm not here because I want to be. I'm here because I'm trying to do the one thing I should have done ten years ago, which is make sure you're safe. Even if I can't be the one who—"

"Jochen."

He stopped.

"I need to ask you something."

"Ask."

"Marysia. She's your daughter. You know this."

"I know."

"What does that make you? In your own mind. A father who abandoned his child? A father who didn't know? What is the story you tell yourself?"

He was quiet for a long time. The coffee sat untouched between them, cooling. Outside the window, the snow had stopped and the sky was the grey-white of old porcelain.

"I tell myself," he said, slowly, "that you made the right choice. That I would have been a terrible father. That the child is better without me. That the things I've done in the last ten years—the things I've been part of—are things that no child should have a father who has done them. I tell myself that you saved her from me."

"And do you believe it?"

"I don't know what I believe anymore. I stopped knowing what I believe about anything the day I saw you in the street. Before that, I had a story. The story was: I'm a good man doing necessary work in a difficult time. I'm serving my country. I'm not responsible for the excesses of the regime. I'm—" He looked up at her. His eyes were bright. "It was a very convincing story. I told it every day for ten years. And then I saw you. And the story fell apart. Like it was made of paper. And everything underneath it—everything I'd been trying to bury—came rushing out."

"What was underneath it?"

"Guilt." The word was barely a whisper. "Guilt. About you. About the child. About every choice I made that led me to a stairwell in Warsaw where a Polish woman I loved looked at me as though I were a stranger. I have been carrying that guilt for ten years, Helena. It is the heaviest thing I have ever carried. Heavier than the uniform. Heavier than the rank. Heavier than the knowledge of what I've watched happen in this city and done nothing to stop."

Helena looked at him. She felt the crack in the wall widen. She felt the things inside—love, grief, fury, longing—pressing against the fissure, trying to get out.

"You watched," she said. "You *watched*. You were here when they liquidated the ghetto. You were here when they burned the hospital. You were here when they—" She stopped. Her voice was shaking. "You were here and you did nothing."

"I did some things."

"What things?"

"Small things. A release here. A transfer there. A word in the right ear at the right time. Nothing that mattered. Nothing that—" He looked at the table. "Nothing that would have changed anything."

"But you signed the paper for Marysia."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because she's my daughter."

"No. That's what you tell yourself. That's the story. Why did you *really* sign it?"

He looked at her. His eyes were those grey-blue lakes, frozen over, and beneath the ice she could see something moving—something dark and desperate and alive.

"Because it was the first thing I've done in ten years that was purely for someone else. Not for my family. Not for my career. Not for my safety. For you. For her. For the child I didn't know existed and the woman I never stopped loving. I signed it because, for one moment, I was able to be the man I should have been. And it felt—" His voice broke. "It felt like breathing for the first time."

Helena closed her eyes. The café was silent around them—the other patrons absorbed in their performances of normalcy, the waitress behind the counter wiping a glass with a rag that was dirtier than the glass. Outside, the street was quiet. The snow had stopped. The world was holding its breath.

She opened her eyes. She looked at Jochen. She looked at the man who had broken her heart and then spent ten years pretending the pieces didn't exist. She looked at the father of her child. She looked at the enemy. She looked at the only person in the world who understood, with the full and terrible clarity of shared experience, what it meant to love someone across the wreckage of everything.

"I need to go," she said.

"I understand."

"I don't understand. I don't understand any of this. I came here for the papers. I'm taking the papers. And I am going home to my daughter. And I am not—" She stood. The chair scraped against the floor. "I am not doing this again. Do you hear me? This is the last time. This café. This conversation. After this, you are a ghost. You released my daughter. You gave me papers. The debt is settled. There is nothing more between us."

He nodded. He did not argue. He did not reach for her. He simply sat and watched her with those eyes that held, in their grey-blue depths, a decade of unspoken things.

"Helena," he said, as she turned to leave.

She stopped.

"If you ever need anything. Anything at all. You know where to find me."

She looked at him. She wanted to say something—something final, something cutting, something that would close the door so completely that no draft could get through. But the words wouldn't come. The fury was still there, but beneath it, like a river beneath ice, was something else. Something she did not want to name. Something that had been waiting, patiently, for ten years, and that was now, finally, beginning to thaw.

She said nothing. She walked out of the café and into the street and did not look back.

## CHAPTER SEVEN

### The Threshold

The weeks that followed the café meeting were a period of terrible, grinding tension. Helena went about her life with the mechanical precision of a woman who has divided herself into compartments that cannot be allowed to communicate. By day, she worked—mending clothes for neighbors, translating documents for the underground, performing the endless small acts of survival that constituted existence in occupied Warsaw. By night, she lay awake and listened to Marysia breathing in the next room and thought about a man who was, against every rational imperative, becoming more present in her mind rather than less.

She told herself it was the shock. The encounter had been a trauma—a detonation that had scattered the carefully arranged furniture of her inner life and left her picking through the wreckage, trying to remember what had belonged where. It was natural, she told herself, that the debris would keep surfacing. It was natural that she would think about him, that his face would appear in her mind at unexpected moments—when she was walking down the street and saw a man with a similar gait, when she was reading and a turn of phrase reminded her of his voice, when she looked at Marysia and saw, in the set of the girl's jaw or the shape of her hands, the ghost of the man he had been.

It was natural. It was not a relapse. It was not a betrayal. It was the mind's way of processing a wound that had been sealed and was now, ten years later, being forced open again.

But the mind, Helena was learning, was not content to merely process. The mind, given enough time and enough unresolved material, began to construct narratives. It began to ask questions that had no safe answers. *What if he had not chosen his family? What if he had followed her to Poland? What if the war had not come, or had come differently, or had not found them?* These questions were not useful. They were not productive. They were the mind's way of filling the silence with fiction, and Helena was too disciplined—too hardened by four years of occupation—to indulge in fiction.

And yet.

And yet.

---

He came to the café again. Two weeks after their meeting, on a Tuesday, she walked in at noon and found him already there, at the same table, with two cups of coffee and a look of such naked hope that she felt her carefully constructed defenses shiver.

"I didn't ask you to come," she said, sitting down.

"I know."

"I told you—"

"I know what you told me. I'm not here to argue. I'm here because I needed to see you. To know you were safe. I won't stay long."

She looked at him. He was thinner than he had been in the processing office. There were shadows under his eyes. He had not shaved that morning—she could see the faint blue of stubble beneath his jaw. He looked like a man who was not sleeping.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I'm not sleeping."

"That's your problem, not mine."

"I know."

Silence. The coffee steamed between them. The café was nearly empty—the midday lull, the hour when the occupied world retreated behind its curtains and its doors and its careful silences.

"Why are you here, Jochen?"

"Because I don't know how to stop." He said it simply, without self-pity, as though reporting a medical condition. "I've tried. I told myself that the papers were enough. That the release was enough. That I had done what I could and now I should withdraw. I told myself this for two weeks. I woke up every morning and I told myself: *she is safe, you have done your part, now let her go*. And every morning I got up and came here, hoping I would see you walk through that door."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I sit here for an hour and I go back to my billet and I try again tomorrow."

"That's insane."

"I know."

"You could be discovered. Someone could see you. A Polish woman meeting with a German officer—"

"No one sees me. I come in the back. The owner knows me. I leave through the kitchen. I am, if nothing else, careful."

She stared at him. Something in her chest shifted—not the crack, which was already there, but something deeper. A tectonic movement. The realization that this man—who had broken her heart, who had abandoned her, who was now wearing the uniform of the power that was destroying her country—was sitting in a café every day at noon, hoping to see her, unable to stop himself from hoping, unable to walk away.

And the terrible, unforgivable truth was that she was coming too. That she had walked into this café knowing he would be here. That she had, in the two weeks since their last meeting, thought of nothing else. That the compartment she had built for him—the sealed room, the locked drawer, the place where she kept the things she could not deal with—was not working. That he was leaking out of it, seeping through the cracks, filling the rooms of her mind with his presence like gas filling a house.

She was in danger. Not from him. From herself. From the part of her that remembered, with a clarity that was almost sexual in its intensity, the feeling of his body against hers, his mouth on hers, his hands in her hair. From the part of her that had spent ten years without being touched, without being held, without the simple animal comfort of another body near hers in the dark. From the part of her that was, despite everything—despite the war, despite the uniform, despite the child, despite the decade of silence and fury and grief—still in love with him.

"You're a fool," she said.

"I know."

"A dangerous fool."

"I know."

"If anyone sees you—"

"I know."

She looked at him. He looked at her. The café was silent. The snow was falling again—a light, drifting snow that settled on the windowsill and melted without sound.

"I can't do this," she said. "I can't sit here every day and pretend this is normal. I can't—"

"I'm not asking you to pretend anything. I'm asking you to let me—"

"What? What are you asking, Jochen? What do you want from me?"

The question hung in the air. He looked at her with those eyes—the grey-blue winter eyes that had, ten years ago, looked at her across a seminar table and changed the trajectory of her life. And in those eyes she saw, clearly, for the first time, the full extent of what he was feeling: not just love, not just guilt, not just desire, but a loneliness so vast and so deep that it was almost geological—a loneliness that had been carved into him by ten years of living a lie, of wearing a mask, of performing a role that did not fit, of suppressing everything he actually felt and believed in order to survive in a machine that did not tolerate dissent.

He was lonely in a way that only a person who has been pretending can be lonely. And she recognized that loneliness because she had been living it too—not the pretending, exactly, but the performance. The performance of being fine. The performance of being over it. The performance of being a woman who had built a life from nothing and was satisfied with it and did not need anything more.

They were both performing. And in this café, at this table, in this occupied city in the winter of 1943, the performances were, for the first time, falling away.

"I want to see you," he said. "That's all. I want to see you and talk to you and know that you exist. I don't want anything else. I have no right to anything else."

Helena was quiet for a long time. She thought about Marysia, asleep in the flat. She thought about the resistance cell, the work, the danger. She thought about the vows she had made and the walls she had built and the careful architecture of a life designed to exclude this man. She thought about all of it, and she weighed it against the thing that was pressing against the inside of her chest like a fist trying to break through a window—the thing that had been waiting for ten years, the thing that was not rational or useful or safe, but that was, nonetheless, the most real thing she had ever felt.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Same time. And Jochen—"

"Yes?"

"Don't come in through the kitchen. Come in through the front. If someone sees you, let them see you. A German officer having coffee is not a crime. It's what you people do."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You're being cruel."

"I'm being practical. If you're going to do this—whatever *this* is—we do it carefully. But not so carefully that it looks suspicious. That's how people get caught. By looking like they're hiding."

He nodded. He understood. She could see it in his eyes—the relief, the gratitude, the dawning comprehension that she was not pushing him away. That she was, in fact, doing something much more dangerous and much more terrible: she was letting him in.

---

They met every day for two weeks. The café became their place—not exclusively, not obviously, but with the quiet regularity of a ritual that neither party acknowledged but both parties needed. They talked. They talked about everything and nothing—the war, obliquely, in the careful coded language of the occupied; the city, which they both knew intimately but from completely different perspectives; the weather, the food, the small absurdities of daily life that persisted even in the worst of times. They talked around the center of things—the love, the child, the decade of silence— circling it like planets circling a star they could not approach without burning.

And slowly, imperceptibly, the distance between them closed.

It happened in stages, each one so small that Helena could pretend, afterward, that it hadn't happened at all. First, his hand on the table moved closer to hers. Then, his fingers brushed hers—once, accidentally, and then deliberately, and then continuously, the two hands resting on the wood between the coffee cups like a declaration that neither of them was ready to speak. Then, one afternoon, he reached across and took her hand, and she did not pull away, and the world did not end, and the war continued, and the snow fell, and the café remained silent around them, and nothing changed, and everything changed.

During those weeks, the occupation pressed against the city with a new and particular cruelty. February gave way to a cold that was not merely meteorological but political—a coldness that seeped from the walls of the Gestapo headquarters, from the barracks on Aleja Szucha, from the processing centers where civilians were sorted like parcels and dispatched to destinations that were, for the most part, final. The ghetto was being liquidated—block by block, family by family—and the sounds of the liquidation carried across the city like the distant thunder of a storm that was approaching but had not yet arrived.

Helena heard the sounds from the flat. At night, lying in the dark with Marysia beside her, she could hear the gunfire from the ghetto—the rapid staccato of automatic weapons, the deeper boom of grenades, the occasional scream that rose above the noise and then stopped, abruptly, like a candle being extinguished. She lay awake and listened and held her daughter and thought about the people behind those sounds—people who were being driven from their homes, who were being herded into cattle cars, who were being shot in the streets or in the courtyards or in the places that had once been their living rooms and their kitchens and their children's bedrooms. She thought about them and she felt the weight of their suffering as a physical thing—a weight that pressed on her chest and made it difficult to breathe, a weight that was both hers and not hers, both personal and impersonal, both the specific agony of a woman who had loved a German and the general agony of a nation that was being methodically destroyed.

Marysia asked questions. She was nine, and she was smart, and she could hear the sounds from the ghetto, and she understood, in the way that children of the occupation understood things, that something terrible was happening that she could not fully comprehend.

"Mama. What's happening?"

"People are being hurt, kochanie."

"By the Germans?"

"Yes."

"Can we help them?"

Helena closed her eyes. She thought about the resistance—the work she did, the documents she forged, the messages she carried. She thought about the people who were fighting, dying, resisting in the only way they could. She thought about Jochen—a German who was, in his way, resisting the thing he was part of, though the distinction was so fine, so gossamer, so easily dissolved that it barely qualified as resistance at all.

"We help the best way we can," she said. "We survive. We remember. We don't let them take everything."

"Can they take everything?"

"No. They can take things. They can take houses and clothes and food. But they can't take—" She stopped. She searched for the word. "They can't take who we are. Inside. Where it matters."

Marysia considered this. Then she said: "Even if they take the person I love?"

The question was so direct, so precise, so devastating in its simplicity that Helena could not answer it. She held her daughter and she stared at the ceiling and she thought about the man who was, at this moment, sitting in a requisitioned apartment three streets away, writing letters he would never send, carrying a guilt that was both his and not his, and she felt the answer to Marysia's question in every cell of her body:

*Yes. They can take that too. They can take the person you love. And you will survive it. And the surviving will be the hardest thing you ever do.*

She did not say this. She said: "No one is going to take the person you love. I promise."

It was a lie. It was a necessary lie, the kind of lie that parents tell to protect children from truths they are not yet ready to carry. Helena told it without hesitation, without guilt, because the alternative—telling the truth, telling her nine-year-old daughter that the world was a place where love could be taken from you at any moment, where the person you loved most could disappear into a roundup or a prison or a grave—was not an alternative at all. It was cruelty. And Helena Wojtera was many things, but she was not cruel. Not to her daughter. Never to her daughter.

The first time he touched her face was on a Thursday. They were walking—she had agreed, against her better judgment, to walk with him through the botanical gardens, which were open to civilians in the afternoon and which offered, in the bare winter trees and the frozen paths, a kind of privacy that the café could not. They were walking side by side, not touching, the snow crunching under their boots, and he had said something—she couldn't remember what, something about a book he'd been reading, something irrelevant and small—and she had laughed, a real laugh, the first real laugh she had given in months, and he had stopped and turned to her and his hand had come up and his fingers had touched her cheek and the world had tilted on its axis.

"Your skin," he said, very quietly. "It's cold."

"It's winter."

"Let me—" He didn't finish. His hand cupped her face. His thumb brushed her cheekbone. The touch was so gentle, so tentative, that it was almost unbearable—the touch of a man who knows he has no right to touch and who is touching anyway because the alternative is to spend another ten years without it.

Helena stood very still. She could feel the heat of his palm against her skin. She could feel the roughness of his fingertips—calluses from the pen, from the papers, from the work of occupation. She could feel, beneath the touch, the weight of everything it contained: ten years of absence, ten years of silence, ten years of a love that had been sealed in a room and was now, finally, being allowed to breathe.

She closed her eyes. She leaned into his hand. It was the smallest movement—perhaps a centimeter, perhaps less—but it was the most significant movement of her life. It was the movement of a woman who had spent ten years holding herself rigid and who was now, for one moment, allowing herself to soften.

He drew her toward him. His other hand found her waist. She could feel the warmth of him through the layers of clothing—coat, dress, blouse, the woolen underthings her mother had knitted. He was solid. He was real. He was here.

"Helena—" His voice was rough. "I can't—"

"I know."

"I don't deserve—"

"I know."

"Let me—"

She looked up at him. His face was close—close enough that she could see the individual flecks in his eyes, close enough that she could feel his breath on her lips, close enough that the distance between them was not a distance at all but a question.

She kissed him.

It was not a gentle kiss. It was not tentative or careful or controlled. It was ten years of fury and grief and longing and desire compressed into a single point of contact and released with the force of an explosion. Her hands found the lapels of his coat and pulled him closer, and his arms came around her and held her—the way he had held her on the bridge, the way he had held her in the small flat, the way he had held her in a thousand moments that she had spent a decade trying to forget and that her body had, apparently, remembered perfectly.

His mouth was the same. The taste of him was the same—coffee and tobacco and something underneath, something that was uniquely *him*, a flavor that was encoded in her memory at a level deeper than thought. Her tongue found his, and the kiss deepened, and the botanical garden disappeared, and the war disappeared, and the occupation disappeared, and the child sleeping in the flat three streets away disappeared, and there was only this: two bodies pressed together in a winter garden, holding each other as though the holding were the only thing in the world that mattered, which, for the length of the kiss, it was.

When they broke apart, they were both breathing hard. Helena's face was flushed. Jochen's eyes were dark. The snow continued to fall around them, indifferent to human emotion, performing its ancient function of covering things in white.

"We shouldn't," Helena said.

"I know."

"This is—"

"I know."

"I have to go."

"I know."

But she didn't go. She stood there, in his arms, in the winter garden, and she felt the war pressing against the edges of the moment like water pressing against a dam, and she knew that the dam would not hold, that the moment would not last, that the world would reassert itself and the consequences would come and the things she had built would shake and possibly crumble. And she knew, with a clarity that was both terrifying and liberating, that she didn't care.

"Come home with me," she said.

He stared at her. "Helena—"

"My daughter is with Pani Kowalska. She'll be there until evening. No one will see you. The flat is—" She stopped. She was explaining, and the explanations were the last defense of a woman who was trying to maintain some fiction of control over a situation that had, irreversibly, passed beyond her control. "Come home with me, Jochen. Please."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and took her hand, and they walked out of the botanical garden and through the snow-covered streets and up the stairs of the building on ulica Mokotowska and into the flat that smelled of coal dust and old plaster and the particular odor of a woman who had lived alone with her child for nine years and who was now, for the first time since a winter morning in Munich a decade ago, opening the door to the man she had never stopped loving.

---

The flat was small and cold and lit by a single candle because the electricity was unreliable and the oil for the lamps was expensive and difficult to obtain. Helena lit the candle and set it on the table and stood in the center of the room and watched Jochen take it in—watched his eyes move across the peeling wallpaper, the narrow bed, the small stove, the curtain that separated the sleeping alcove from the living space. ## CHAPTER EIGHT

### The Web Tightens

Morning came the way mornings came in occupied Warsaw: reluctantly, greyly, with the particular quality of light that suggested the sun was apologizing for returning. Helena woke to the sound of the building stirring—the pipes groaning, a door closing somewhere below, the faint crackle of a radio being turned on in a neighboring flat. She lay still, her body aching in places that had not ached in ten years, and she was aware, before she opened her eyes, of the weight beside her.

Jochen was asleep. He lay on his side, facing her, one arm draped across the narrow bed as though even in sleep he was reaching for something. His face, in the grey morning light, was young. The lines had softened. The jaw had unclenched. He looked, for the first time since she had seen him in the street, like a man at peace.

She watched him. She watched him with the cold, clear attention of a woman who knows that what she is watching is temporary—that the peace will not last, that the morning is a lie, that the world will reassert itself as soon as the man beside her opens his eyes and remembers where he is and who she is and what they have done.

She got up. She dressed. She moved to the kitchen alcove and lit the stove and set water to heat. She performed the morning ritual with the mechanical precision of a woman who needs the familiarity of routine to keep from falling apart. Her hands were steady. Her face was composed. Inside, she was a landscape after an earthquake—shifted, fractured, fundamentally altered.

Jochen woke while she was making the barley water. She heard him stir, heard the creak of the bed, heard the moment of disorientation that always accompanies waking in an unfamiliar place. Then she heard his breathing change as he remembered.

"I know."

"Before anyone—"

"Helena. I need you to—"

She turned. His face was close. She could see the fear in his eyes—not fear of discovery, though that was there too, but a deeper fear. The fear of a man who has, for the first time in ten years, allowed himself to feel something real and who is now terrified that the feeling will be taken away.

"This changes nothing. About the war. About the occupation. About what you are and what I am. We are on opposite sides, Jochen. Last night doesn't change that. Tonight doesn't change that. Whatever this is—whatever we're doing—it exists in a space between the war and us. It doesn't reach into the war. It doesn't change the war."

He nodded. He understood. But she could see, in his eyes, that he didn't agree. He believed—foolishly, desperately, with the conviction of a man who needs it to be true—that what they had was strong enough to reach into the war. That love, across the wreckage, could change the architecture of the world.

She didn't believe it. She couldn't afford to. But she also couldn't deny what had happened. And so they stood in the kitchen alcove of a flat in occupied Warsaw, in the grey light of a winter morning, and they looked at each other with the expression of two people who know that what they have is both the most important and the most impossible thing in the world.

He left by the back stairs. She heard him descend, heard the back door open and close, heard his footsteps on the cobblestones of the alley, and then silence. She locked the door. She bolted it. She went to Marysia's alcove and stood in the doorway and watched the girl sleep.

Marysia was curled on her side, one hand under her cheek, her breathing deep and regular. She looked, in sleep, like a child. Like a normal child sleeping in a normal bed in a normal world. Helena looked at her and felt the weight of what she had done settle on her shoulders like a physical thing.

She had brought a German officer into the flat where her daughter slept. She had let him into the room where Marysia's toys were stacked on a shelf—handmade things, a cloth doll, a wooden horse, a book of fairy tales with torn pages. She had let him into the space where her daughter's childhood lived, and she had taken him into her bed, and she had—

She stopped the thought. She would not go there. She would not allow herself to evaluate what she had done with the cold moral calculus of the resistance. She would not call it what the resistance would call it—collaboration, betrayal, the sleeping with the enemy. She would not. Because she knew, with a certainty that preceded thought, that what had happened in the flat last night was not collaboration. It was something else. Something older. Something that had nothing to do with the war and everything to do with the fact that two people had loved each other once and had been torn apart by the world and had found each other again in the wreckage and could not—could not, despite every rational argument to the contrary—let go.

## CHAPTER NINE

### The Reckoning

She made her decision three days later.

It came to her not as a choice but as a recognition—like recognizing a face in a crowd, like hearing a word you'd been trying to remember. She would not use Jochen. She would not become what Marek wanted her to become: a weapon aimed at the heart of a man who loved her. She could not. She had spent ten years building walls, and the last thing she would do was use those walls as a trap.

But she would not leave the resistance either. She had given four years to the cause. She had forged documents that had saved lives. She had carried messages through streets where a wrong step meant death. She had buried friends. She had watched her country burn. She was not going to walk away.

She would find a third way.

The third way was this: she would warn Jochen. She would tell him about the sweep—the planned operation in Mokotow that Marek had mentioned. She would give him the information that would allow him to protect himself, to avoid being in a position where his name would be connected to anything that went wrong. And in doing so, she would give him something he could use—not intelligence that would harm Germany, but information that would keep him safe. It was a compromise. It was, by the strict standards of the resistance, inadequate. But it was all she could give without crossing a line she was not willing to cross.

Her father died in 1942. It was the winter of the Great Action—the mass deportation from Warsaw that sent three hundred thousand Jews to Treblinka in the space of two months. Helena's father was not Jewish, but he was a professor, and the Germans had a particular hatred for professors. They understood, in the way that all tyrants understand, that the people who teach are the people who remember, and that memory is the enemy of erasure.

He was arrested on a January morning—arrested in the street, in broad daylight, by a patrol that was sweeping the university district for intelligentsia. Helena learned of it from a neighbor who had seen the patrol drag him from the doorway of the building where he had lived for thirty years. He had been carrying a bag of books. He had dropped them in the struggle, and the books had lain in the snow—Heine, Mickiewicz, a volume of Plato—while the patrol shoved him into the truck and drove away.

Helena went to the Pawiak prison the next day. The Pawiak—the infamous Gestapo prison on Dzielna Street, where the walls were thick enough to absorb the sounds of screaming and where the cells were so overcrowded that prisoners took turns sleeping, lying on the floor in shifts, the rest standing pressed against the walls. She went to the Pawiak and she asked to see her father and she was told, by a clerk with a bored face and ink-stained fingers, that Professor Wojtera was not in the Pawiak. He had been transferred. To another facility. For processing.

Helena knew what "processing" meant. Everyone in Warsaw knew what "processing" meant. It meant Palmiry—the forest outside the city where the Germans conducted their mass executions. It meant the ditches, the blindfolds, the bullets to the back of the head. It meant the end of things.

She waited three weeks. She went back to the Pawiak every three days. Each time, the same answer: *not here, transferred, processing*. On the fourth visit, the clerk—a different clerk, a younger one, with the uncertain eyes of someone who had not yet been entirely dehumanized by the work—leaned across the counter and said, in a voice barely above a whisper: "Your father is dead. He was executed at Palmiry on the twentieth of January. His body is in the forest. Do not come back here. They are watching you."

Helena walked out of the Pawiak and into the street and she did not cry. She walked home. She told her mother. Her mother—a woman who had been strong for thirty years of marriage, who had raised a daughter, who had kept a household through hyperinflation and depression and war, who had never, in all that time, shown weakness—her mother collapsed. Not dramatically. Not with a cry or a faint. She simply sat down in her chair and she did not get up again. She stopped eating. She stopped speaking. She looked at the wall with the steady, vacant gaze of a person who has decided, with the quiet finality of the very old, that the world is no longer a place worth inhabiting.

She died in April. Pneumonia, the doctor said. But Helena knew. Her mother had died of grief. She had died because the man she had loved for forty years had been taken from her, and the world had not ended, and the sun had continued to rise, and the Germans had continued their work, and the sheer, obscene persistence of the world in the face of her loss was more than she could bear.

Helena buried them both—her father in the forest at Palmiry, where his body lay in a mass grave with two hundred other executed intellectuals; her mother in the Powązki Cemetery, where the graves of the old Warsaw families were being systematically destroyed by the occupiers to make room for their own dead. She stood at her mother's grave—a small, unmarked plot, the earth still fresh—and she felt the last of her family dissolve into the earth, and she was alone.

Alone except for Marysia. Marysia, who was eight years old, who did not understand why her grandfather was gone and her grandmother was gone and why the world had become a place where people disappeared without explanation and without return. Helena held her daughter and she made a vow—not the old vow, the vow of silence and secrecy, but a new vow, a vow of survival. She would survive. She would keep Marysia alive. She would carry her through the war, through the occupation, through whatever came next, and she would deliver her to the other side of it—battered, scarred, traumatized, perhaps, but alive. *Alive.* That was all she wanted. That was all the war had left her.

And then, six months later, a German officer walked out of the snow on ulica Mokotowska and looked at her with grey-blue eyes and said her name, and the new vow—to survive, to carry her child to safety, to live—suddenly became complicated in ways she had not anticipated and could not control.

She told him about her father on a cold afternoon in late February, during one of their walks through the botanical garden. They were sitting on a bench near the frozen lake, and she had been quiet for a long time, staring at the ice, and he had asked—gently, without pressure—what she was thinking about, and she had told him. About the arrest. About the Pawiak. About Palmiry. About the books in the snow.

Jochen listened. He did not speak. He did not offer comfort—the empty, useless comfort of *I'm sorry* or *it wasn't my fault* or *not all of us*. He listened, and when she was finished, he said:

"My father sent my uncle to Palmiry."

Helena looked at him. "What?"

"My father's brother. Uncle Friedrich. He was a professor of mathematics at Heidelberg. He made remarks—critical remarks about the regime—in a seminar. A student reported him. The Gestapo arrested him. My father—my father could have intervened. He had connections. He could have pulled strings. He chose not to. He said Friedrich had brought it on himself. That a von Brandt does not protect dissidents." Jochen's voice was flat. "Friedrich was executed in '40. At Dachau. Not Palmiry, but the same principle. The same machinery."

Silence. Helena stared at him. This was the first time he had told her anything about his family's involvement in the violence of the regime—anything that acknowledged, even indirectly, the blood on their hands.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you need to know. You need to know that the von Brandt family is not merely politically aligned with the regime. It is *complicit* in its crimes. My uncle died because my father chose not to save him. And I—every day that I wear this uniform, every day that I serve this army, every day that I do nothing to stop what is happening in this city—I am choosing not to save anyone else. I am my father's son, Helena. That is what I am."

"No." Helena's voice was sharp. "You are not your father's son. Your father chose his reputation over his brother's life. You chose Marysia's safety over your own. Those are not the same choice."

"They feel the same."

"They're not. The difference is this: your father made his choice and lived with it comfortably. He went on being a respected industrialist. He attended parties. He voted for the party. He never, not once, expressed regret. You—" She looked at him. "You are in a sewer in your own mind, Jochen. You have been since the day I left Munich. You have been punishing yourself for ten years. And that punishment—that guilt—that is what makes you different from your father. Because he never punished himself. He never even noticed he needed to."

He looked at her. His eyes were those grey-blue lakes, and in them she saw something she had not seen before: the faintest flicker of hope. Not happiness. Not absolution. But the beginning of the idea that he might, perhaps, be capable of something other than guilt—that he might, in the wreckage of his choices, find something worth preserving.

She took his hand. She held it. They sat on the bench in the botanical garden, and the ice on the lake was thick and grey, and the trees were bare, and the city was burning somewhere beyond the trees, and they held hands and said nothing, because sometimes the only thing that words are good for is ruining the silence.

She told him at the café. They were sitting at their table, in their corner, the routine so established now that the waitress brought two coffees without asking. She spoke quietly, her lips barely moving, in the coded language of the occupied: oblique references, half-sentences, the grammar of implication.

"There are going to be operations. Soon. In my district. Large ones. I don't know when, but—"

Jochen's face went white. "When did you hear this?"

"It doesn't matter. What matters is that you know. That you're careful. That you—"

"Helena." His voice was urgent. "You need to leave. You need to leave Warsaw. Now. Before—"

"I'm not leaving."

"You have to. If the sweep is coming—if the district is going to be—"

"My daughter is not going anywhere. Not in this weather, not without papers for the countryside, not without—"

"Then take her to the countryside. I'll arrange—"

"Jochen." She leaned forward. Her eyes held his. "Listen to me. I'm not running. I've spent four years running. I'm done running. If the sweep comes, I'll deal with it the way I've dealt with everything else: I'll stand my ground and I'll survive. That's what survivors do."

He looked at her. The grey-blue eyes were dark with something she couldn't name—fear, yes, but also something else. Admiration, perhaps. Or grief. Or the recognition that the woman he loved was a force of nature that could not be controlled, only witnessed.

"You're impossible," he said.

"I'm Polish."

He almost smiled. It was the same non-smile he'd given in the processing office—the ghost of a smile, the shadow of the young man who used to laugh in seminars. "You're going to get yourself killed."

"Probably. But not today."

---

The sweep came on a Tuesday in early March.

Helena knew it was coming—she could feel it in the city's atmosphere, in the increased patrols, in the way the Germans moved through the streets with a new intensity, like hunters who had caught a scent. She spent the morning preparing: documents in order, bags packed, escape routes mapped. Marysia sat on the bed and watched her mother with the steady, unblinking attention of a child who has learned that the best response to danger is observation.

"Mama, are we going to hide?"

"Yes, kochanie. We're going to hide."

"Where?"

"The cellar. Pani Iwanowska's cellar, two buildings down. She said we could use it if anything happened."

"Is it safe?"

"It's safer than here."

Marysia nodded. She stood. She picked up her small bag—the one she had packed herself, containing a change of clothes, a book, a cloth doll, and a crust of bread wrapped in a napkin. She was nine years old, and she was ready.

They went out at midday, moving quickly through the streets with their heads down and their bags clutched to their chests. The street was tense—people moving with the careful nonchalance of the hunted, eyes darting, shoulders hunched. A patrol passed them on the corner—a truck full of soldiers, their rifles visible, their faces blank with the particular blankness of men doing work they have been told is necessary.

They reached Pani Iwanowska's building. The old woman was waiting at the door, her face a mask of grim determination. She ushered them into the cellar—a low, dark space that smelled of earth and old wine and the particular odor of things hidden for a long time. There were others there: a woman with two children, an old man with a cane, a young couple holding hands. All of them quiet. All of them waiting.

Helena sat on the stone floor with Marysia in her lap and waited.

The sounds came from above: boots on cobblestone, shouted orders, the crack of a door being forced open. The cellar vibrated with the footsteps of the patrol passing overhead. Marysia pressed her face into Helena's chest and did not make a sound.

The sounds moved away. The vibrations lessened. The cellar was quiet.

Helena waited. She waited for an hour. Two hours. The woman with the children was crying silently. The old man's hands were shaking on his cane. The young couple had their eyes closed.

Then: a different sound. Not boots. Not orders. Gunfire. Close. Three shots, rapid, then silence.

Marysia's body went rigid against Helena's chest. The woman with the children put a hand over her youngest child's mouth. The old man closed his eyes.

Helena held her daughter and stared at the cellar ceiling and listened to the sounds of the occupation doing its work above her head. She had heard these sounds before. She would hear them again. They were the sounds of the world she lived in, and they were, after four years, almost—but not quite—familiar. The almost was where the terror lived. The almost was where you could still be surprised, could still be shocked, could still feel the full force of the violence as though it were the first time.

The sounds stopped. The patrol moved on. The city resumed its uneasy silence.

Helena waited another hour. Then she stood, lifted Marysia, and moved toward the stairs.

"Mama, where are we going?"

"Home. I need to check the flat."

They climbed the stairs. They emerged into the street. The street was empty—emptier than usual, the emptiness that follows a sweep, when the people who have not been taken are hiding and the people who have been taken are gone. The air smelled of smoke.

They walked to the building. The front door was open—forced open, the lock broken. Helena's stomach dropped. She climbed the stairs with Marysia on her back, her legs burning with the effort, and she reached the fourth floor and—

The flat was destroyed.

The door had been kicked in. The furniture was overturned. The mattress was slashed. The stove had been pulled from the wall. The curtains were torn down. The small shelf where Marysia's toys were kept was empty—swept bare, the toys scattered on the floor like casualties of a small war. The papers—the forged papers, the resistance documents, the records of Helena's four years of work—were gone. Every paper in the flat had been taken.

Helena stood in the wreckage and breathed. She breathed in the smell of destruction—the dust of broken plaster, the chemical tang of torn fabric, the faint residual odor of coal smoke from the stove that had been dragged away. She breathed, and she did not scream, and she did not cry, because screaming and crying were luxuries, and she could not afford luxuries.

She set Marysia down. The girl was looking at the room with wide, dark eyes.

"Mama. Our things—"

"I know, kochanie. I know."

"Is it because of him? The officer?"

Helena knelt in front of her daughter. She took Marysia's face in her hands. The girl's skin was cold. Her eyes were the eyes of a child who has seen too much, who is aging by the hour, whose childhood is being consumed by a war that does not care about children.

"It's not because of him," Helena said. "It's because of the war. The war does this. It takes things. It breaks things. But we are not broken. We are still here. Do you understand? We are still here."

Marysia nodded. But her eyes said something different. Her eyes said: *How long can we still be here?*

They slept that night in Pani Iwanowska's cellar, on blankets the old woman had brought down, and Helena lay awake listening to Marysia's breathing and thinking about the papers that had been taken. The resistance papers. The forgeries. The documents that identified the members of the cell, the safe houses, the meeting points. If the Gestapo examined them—if they were good enough to crack the codes, to follow the leads, to trace the network back to its source—then the cell was compromised. Not just Helena. Not just her. Everyone. Marek. Zofia. Tomasz. Every person who had passed through the pharmacy basement in the last four years.

She had to warn them. She had to get to Marek before the Gestapo got to him.

She left before dawn. She told Pani Iwanowska to keep Marysia hidden and not to open the door for anyone. The old woman nodded and crossed herself and said: "Go with God, child."

Helena walked through the pre-dawn streets—dark, empty, the snow still on the ground, the sky a deep blue-black that was neither night nor morning. She walked quickly, her head down, her collar up, performing the posture of the invisible woman that every occupied person learns to wear.

She reached the pharmacy. The door was locked. She knocked—the coded knock, three short and one long. No answer. She knocked again. Silence.

She stepped back and looked at the building. The windows were dark. There was no sign of life. And then she saw it—the thing her body recognized before her mind did: the door frame. The wood around the lock was splintered. Not from the inside. From the outside. The door had been forced.

She turned and walked away. She walked quickly, without looking back, because looking back was the behavior of someone who had been seen, and she had not been seen. She walked three blocks, turned a corner, leaned against a wall, and breathed.

They were gone. The cell was gone. Marek, Zofia, Tomasz—gone. Taken, or scattered, or dead. She didn't know which. She would never know which. The occupation did not provide closure. It provided only absence, and the silence that filled the space where people used to be.

She walked home. She climbed the stairs. She entered the wrecked flat. She sat on the slashed mattress. And she wept.

Not for Marek, though she wept for him too. Not for Zofia or Tomasz or Pani Irena. Not for the resistance, though the resistance was gone, its network in this district destroyed, its work undone. She wept for herself. For the life she had built in this flat—the life of mending and forgery and resistance, the life that had given her suffering a purpose. That life was over now. The papers were gone. The cell was gone. The work was gone.

She was, for the first time in four years, alone.

---

Jochen found her the next day.

He came to the flat—he still had the key she had given him in a moment of recklessness she now regretted—and he found her sitting on the floor of the ruined kitchen, staring at the wall.

"Helena." He dropped to his knees beside her. His hands found her shoulders. "What happened? I heard about the sweep—"

"They took the cell. Everyone. Marek, Zofia, Tomasz. All gone. And the papers—everything. Every document I had. Four years of work. Gone."

He held her. She let him hold her. She was past the point of resistance, past the point of pride, past the point of pretending that she could manage this alone. She was a woman who had just lost everything—her work, her network, her purpose—and the only thing she had left was the man beside her, and even he was a thing she could not fully trust, could not fully hold, could not fully have.

"We need to get you out," he said. "Both of you. Today. I'll arrange—"

"No."

"Helena—"

"No. I'm not leaving Warsaw. My daughter is not leaving Warsaw. This is our home. This is where we stay."

"They'll come for you. If they find the papers—if they trace them back—"

"They won't. The papers were good. Tomasz was the best forger in the district. They won't trace back. Not quickly. And by the time they do—"

"They will, Helena. It's only a matter of time."

"Then we have time. And we'll use it."

He looked at her. His face was pale, his eyes dark. He was frightened—not for himself, she could see, but for her. For the child. The fear was real, and it was loving, and it was the most honest thing he had shown her since the war began.

"What do you need?" he asked.

She looked at him. She considered the question. She considered the man. She considered the ten years between them and the war around them and the child sleeping in a cellar three streets away. And she made a decision—not the decision Marek had wanted, not the decision that would have used him as a resource, but a different decision. A decision that was, she knew, equally dangerous and equally compromising.

"I need you to help me rebuild," she said.

He stared at her. "You want me to—"

"The resistance is gone in this district. But it's not gone everywhere. There are other cells. Other networks. If I can reconnect—if I can find a new cell, a new way to work—I can still be useful. But I need papers. I need contacts. I need—" She stopped. She looked at him. "I need someone on the inside."

"You want me to help the resistance."

"I want you to help me survive. If surviving means resisting, then yes. I want you to help me resist."

He was quiet for a long time. She could see the calculation behind his eyes—not a cold calculation, but a desperate one. The calculation of a man who is being asked to choose, finally and irrevocably, between his country and his love. Between the uniform and the woman. Between the machine and the heart.

"If I do this," he said, slowly, "there is no going back. If I'm caught—if anyone finds out—I'm dead. Not imprisoned. Not transferred. Dead. The punishment for aiding the resistance is execution."

"I know."

"And you. If they find out you're connected to me—if they find out about the child—"

"I know."

"They'll kill you both."

"I know."

Silence. The flat was cold around them—the coal was gone, the stove was broken, the winter pressed against the windows like a living thing.

"Yes," he said.

Helena closed her eyes. She felt the weight of the word settle on her shoulders. It was not a light weight. It was the weight of a man's life, a child's safety, and the fragile, terrible, impossible thing that existed between them—a thing that had no name, that had no precedent, that was either the most beautiful or the most stupid thing either of them had ever done.

"Yes," she said. "Then we begin."

## CHAPTER TEN

### What the River Remembers

The spring of 1943 came to Warsaw the way spring comes to all occupied cities: slowly, grudgingly, as though the season itself were reluctant to bring beauty to a place so thoroughly devoted to ugliness. The snow retreated. The Vistula thawed, its dark water moving under the bridges with the indifferent persistence of a river that has outlasted every empire that has tried to claim its banks. The trees in the botanical garden—where Helena and Jochen had first kissed—began to bud, their bare branches reaching toward a sky that was, for the first time in months, occasionally blue.

Helena and Jochen worked in the spaces between the war's attention. It was a dangerous, grinding, exhausting business. Jochen passed information—not military intelligence, never that, but the small, critical details that kept people alive: the timing of roundups, the locations of patrols, the names of informants in the district. Helena relayed this information to a new cell she had found—a smaller group, operating out of Praga, led by a former army sergeant named Kaczmarek who asked no questions about her connections and cared only about results.

They met less frequently now. Not because either of them wanted it that way, but because the danger had escalated to a level that made every meeting a potential death sentence. Jochen was watched—not openly, not yet, but with the subtle attention of an organization that notices when one of its officers begins behaving irregularly. His commanding officer, a Colonel named Brandt—no relation, a coincidence that made Jochen's skin crawl—had called him in twice in the past month for "routine reviews" that were anything but routine.

"He asked me about my political sympathies," Jochen told Helena one evening, in a borrowed room above a tailor's shop on Nowoliпki street that they used as a meeting place. "He asked if I was loyal to the Reich. He asked if there was anything in my past that might—" He stopped. His jaw tightened. "He knows something. I don't know what. But he's watching."

"Can you get out?"

"I've requested a transfer. To the Eastern Front. It's the only plausible reason for leaving Warsaw that wouldn't raise immediate suspicion."

"The Eastern Front." Helena felt the blood drain from her face. "Jochen, that's—"

"Suicide? Probably. But a transfer request is less suspicious than a sudden disappearance. If I just vanish, they'll look for me. If I request a transfer, they'll process it. And if they process it, I'll have maybe two weeks before I'm sent east. Two weeks to—"

"To what?"

"To get you out. Both of you. To get you to a place where they can't reach you."

Helena sat on the edge of the narrow bed in the room above the tailor's shop. She looked at Jochen—this man in his civilian clothes, this man who was not the officer she had met in the street, not the young man she had loved in Munich, but something in between, something new, something forged in the pressure of the war like steel forged in fire. She looked at him and she felt, with a clarity that was almost painful, the totality of what they had built between them—not a life, not a future, not anything so stable or so permanent, but a space. A space between the war and them, a space where love could exist despite the impossibility of its existence, a space that was, in its way, as real as any room they had ever shared.

"I'm not leaving Warsaw," she said.

"Helena—"

"I'm not. My parents are here. My friends—if any of them are still alive—they're here. My daughter's school is here. This is our city. We're not running."

"If you stay and the Gestapo—"

"Then we'll deal with it. We've been dealing with it for four years. We'll deal with it for four more if we have to."

"You can't deal with the Gestapo. No one can deal with the Gestapo."

"No. But you can survive it. That's what I do, Jochen. That's what I've always done. I survive."

He looked at her. He didn't argue. He had learned, over the past weeks, that arguing with Helena Wojtera was like arguing with the Vistula: the river would continue to flow regardless of what you said to it, and the only rational response was to learn to swim.

"I need to tell you something," he said.

"What?"

"My father wrote to me. Last week."

Helena's body went still.

"He's been reassigned. The factory—he's producing components for the war effort. Armaments. He's proud of it. He writes about the family's contribution to the Reich. He writes about how the von Brandt name is associated with excellence and loyalty and—" Jochen stopped. His hands were shaking. "He writes about how he looks forward to the day when I come home with a proper wife. A German wife. A wife who will carry the von Brandt name into the next generation."

Helena said nothing.

"I wrote back," Jochen said. "I told him I was engaged. That I would not be coming home. That I had found someone. That I—" He looked at her. His eyes were bright. "I told him your name. Helena Wojtera. I told him everything. I told him that the child is mine. I told him that I love you. I told him that if he cannot accept this, then the von Brandt name can go to someone who deserves it."

Helena's breath caught. "What did he say?"

"I haven't heard back yet. The letter was only sent two weeks ago. But when he does respond—" A grim smile. "It will not be pleasant."

"Jochen. Your family—"

"My family chose the Reich over me ten years ago. I'm choosing you over the Reich now. It's the least I can do."

She crossed the room. She knelt in front of him. She took his face in her hands—the face she knew from a seminar table, from a bridge, from a narrow bed in a cold flat, from a thousand moments of love and loss and the slow, terrible business of finding each other again in the wreckage. She held his face and she looked into those grey-blue eyes and she saw, for the first time without any reservation, the man he was: not the coward she had accused him of being, not the hero she had never needed him to be, but something in between. A man. A flawed, frightened, loving, stubborn man who had made terrible choices and was now, finally, making a choice that was not terrible. A choice that was, in fact, the only choice that mattered.

"I love you," she said.

He covered her hands with his. "I know."

"I've loved you for ten years. Even when I told myself I didn't. Even when I built walls and made vows and tried to forget. I loved you. And I hate that I love you. I hate it because it makes me vulnerable. It makes me weak. It makes me—" Her voice broke. "It makes me human. And in this war, being human is the most dangerous thing there is."

"I know."

"And I would do it again. I would choose you again. Even knowing what I know. Even knowing where it leads. I would choose you."

He pulled her to him. He held her. The room above the tailor's shop was small and cold and smelled of machine oil and wool, and outside the window the city of Warsaw breathed its occupied breath, and the Vistula flowed under its bridges, and the trees in the botanical garden budded and bloomed, indifferent to the war, indifferent to the lovers, indifferent to everything except the ancient imperative of spring.

---

The end came in August.

Not the end of the war—that was still months away, a distant thunder on the horizon, approaching but not yet arrived. The end of their particular story. The end that Helena had been anticipating since the day she saw Jochen in the street, the end that had been written into the architecture of their situation from the beginning, as inevitable as the final page of a book whose first chapter contained the seeds of its conclusion.

The Uprising began on the first of August. The Polish Home Army, after years of preparation, rose against the German occupation. The city erupted. Barricades went up. Gunfire echoed through the streets. The sky, which had been grey all summer, turned the color of smoke.

Helena was in the flat with Marysia when it began. She heard the first shots from the direction of the city center—the sharp, rapid staccato of small arms fire, followed by the deeper boom of grenades. She ran to the window. The street was chaos—people running, some toward the fighting, some away from it, a woman carrying a baby, a man with a rifle, a boy no older than sixteen with a bandolier of grenades slung across his chest.

"Mama—" Marysia was at the door of the alcove, her face white.

"Get your bag."

"Are we—"

"Get your bag. Now."

They descended the stairs. The building was shaking—distant artillery, the heavier weapons coming into play. The street was a river of people, all moving, all running, the collective flight of a city that has decided, after five years of subjugation, that it would rather die on its feet than live on its knees.

Helena carried Marysia through the crowd. She didn't know where she was going—she only knew that she needed to move, that standing still was death, that the mathematics of survival in an urban battle demanded constant motion, constant adaptation, the refusal to become a fixed point in a landscape of projectiles.

They reached the courtyard of a building on ulica Nowy Swiat—a building Helena knew, where a woman named Pani Maria ran a kitchen that fed resistance fighters. They pounded on the door. Pani Maria opened it—a heavy-set woman of fifty with flour on her hands and fear in her eyes.

"Come in. Come in quickly."

Inside, the courtyard was full of people—civilians, fighters, children, old people, all huddled together in the space between the buildings, looking up at the sky as though the sky might provide answers. The sky was the color of iron. The smoke from the burning city hung low, a pall that turned the afternoon into a premature dusk.

Helena found a corner. She sat with Marysia between her knees and held the girl and watched the war consume the city she had lived in for her entire life.

The fighting raged for three days. Then the Germans began their response—methodical, brutal, overwhelming.

The SS units moved district by district, clearing buildings with the precision of surgeons and the cruelty of butchers. They did not merely kill; they made examples. They lined civilians against walls and shot them in groups of ten, twenty, fifty—the numbers increasing as the days passed, as the German patience with resistance thinned and their fury at the Polish defiance deepened into something that was, in the end, less tactical than theological. This was not war. This was eradication. This was the application of a policy that had been formulated years ago, in the pages of a book written by a failed artist in a Bavarian prison, and that was now being executed with the thoroughness that the German administrative mind brought to all its endeavors.

Helena saw things during those three days that she could not unsee. She saw a woman dragged from a doorway by her hair and shot in the courtyard, the woman's body convulsing on the cobblestones while her baby, still strapped to her chest, screamed with the wordless fury of an infant who does not understand why its mother has stopped moving. She saw a building—six stories, hundreds of people—consumed by flame, the windows bursting outward one by one as the heat reached them, the people inside visible for a moment in the frames of the burning windows, silhouettes against the fire, before the fire consumed them too. She saw a boy—fourteen, fifteen, no older—lying in the street with a bullet wound in his stomach, his hands pressed against the wound, his blood running between his fingers, his eyes open and staring at the sky with the expression of someone who is trying to memorize the shape of the clouds for a test he will never take.

She carried these images in her mind the way she carried everything: silently, without complaint, storing them in the compartment labeled *things I will deal with later*, a compartment that was by now so full that it was beginning to overflow, the things spilling out into the rooms of her consciousness, staining the walls, contaminating the air.

On the third night, she sat in the courtyard with Marysia and looked up at the sky. The sky was the color of blood—not the bright red of fresh blood but the dark, congealed red of old blood, the color of a wound that has been open for a long time and will not close. The smoke from the burning city hung so low that she could taste it—a metallic, chemical taste that coated her tongue and made her gag.

"Mama. Are we going to die?"

Helena looked at her daughter. Marysia's face was thin, dirty, old beyond its years. The girl had seen too much. She had been seeing too much since the roundup, since the camp, since the moment she had learned that her father was a German officer and that the world was not the simple, safe place that children deserve.

"I don't know," Helena said. It was the truth. It was the only truth she had left. "I don't know if we'll die. But I know that we're alive now. And being alive now is the most important thing there is."

"What about after now?"

"After now, we'll deal with it. The way we always deal with it. One moment at a time."

Marysia leaned against her. "I'm tired, Mama."

"I know, kochanie. I know."

Helena held her daughter in the courtyard of a building in a city that was burning, and she looked up at the blood-colored sky, and she felt the weight of the war pressing down on her from all sides—the weight of the occupation, the Uprising, the love, the loss, the impossible situation of being a woman who had loved a German and carried his child and fought against his country and survived it all and was now sitting in the ruins of the city she called home, watching the world end.

On the fourth day, Jochen found them.

He came through the courtyard door in the late afternoon—his uniform gone, his clothes civilian, his face cut and bleeding, a bandage wrapped around his left hand. He looked like a man who had been through a war, which was what he was. He moved through the courtyard with the quick, purposeful stride of a soldier, scanning the crowd, and when he found Helena—sitting in the corner with Marysia against her chest—his face changed. The tension left his shoulders. The calculation left his eyes. He crossed the courtyard in three long strides and dropped to his knees beside them.

"Are you—" he began.

"We're alive." Helena's voice was flat, stripped of everything but fact. "Are you?"

"I'm here. That's the best I can promise."

Marysia looked up at him. The girl's face was dirty, her hair matted, her eyes too large in her thin face. She looked at Jochen with the steady attention of a child who has seen too much and is now cataloguing the survivors.

"Papa," she said.

The word fell into the courtyard like a stone into still water. Jochen's face went through a series of transformations—shock, recognition, grief, joy—before settling on something that was simply and overwhelmingly *love*. The love of a man hearing, for the first time, the word he had spent ten years wanting to hear and had never believed he deserved.

"Marysia," he said. His voice was rough. He reached out and touched the girl's hair—tentatively, as though she might break. "Yes. I'm—" He looked at Helena. "I'm your papa."

Helena closed her eyes. She felt the tears come—hot, sudden, unstoppable. She felt the walls come down. She felt the ten years of silence and fury and grief collapse like a building whose foundations have finally given way, and in the wreckage, in the rubble, in the dust and the noise and the smoke, there was this: a family. A broken, impossible, wartime family, huddled in a courtyard in a burning city, but a family nonetheless.

"We need to get out," Jochen said. "The SS is two blocks east. They're clearing building by building. We have maybe an hour before they reach this courtyard."

"Where can we go?"

"The sewer system. There's an entrance in the basement of the building on the corner. The resistance uses it to move fighters. We can get to the other side of the river—"

"The sewer—"

"It's the only way. The streets are impassable. The bridges are—"

"I know." Helena stood. She lifted Marysia. She looked at Jochen. "Lead the way."

They moved through the building—down the stairs, into the basement, through a door that opened onto a narrow stone corridor that smelled of water and earth and the particular darkness of things that have never seen the sun. The sewer entrance was a hole in the floor—a square opening perhaps two feet across, with an iron ladder descending into blackness.

Jochen went first. He climbed down the ladder and disappeared into the dark. Helena followed, Marysia on her back, the girl's arms locked around her neck, her face pressed against Helena's shoulder. The ladder was slippery with moisture. The darkness below was absolute—a darkness that was not merely the absence of light but the presence of something else, something ancient and animal, the darkness of the earth before the first fire.

They reached the bottom. The sewer tunnel stretched in both directions—a low, narrow passage with a channel of water running down the center, the water black and slow-moving, carrying with it the accumulated waste of a city at war. The smell was overwhelming—fecal, chemical, human, the smell of a city's body turned inside out.

"This way." Jochen took Helena's hand. His other hand held a small flashlight—the only light, a dim beam that illuminated perhaps three meters of tunnel in either direction. They moved through the water, the water rising to their calves, their knees, their thighs. Marysia whimpered against Helena's neck but did not cry. The girl was made of stronger stuff than either of them knew.

They walked for what felt like hours. The tunnel twisted and branched and twisted again. The water grew deeper. At one point it reached their waists, and Helena had to lift Marysia onto her shoulders, and Jochen walked beside her with his hand on her hip, steadying her, his flashlight held high, the beam bouncing off the curved walls of the tunnel and throwing strange, shifting shadows that looked like faces.

They heard the explosions above—the muffled, distant thud of demolition charges, the sound of buildings being brought down, block by block, the systematic destruction of a city. The tunnel vibrated with each blast. Dust fell from the ceiling. The water rippled.

And then—light. A square of grey daylight above them, an opening in the tunnel ceiling that led to a manhole on the surface. Jochen climbed the iron rungs built into the wall and pushed the manhole cover aside. He emerged. He looked around. He came back down.

"The Praga side," he said. "We're across the river. The Germans haven't reached here yet. We can—"

A sound. Behind them. In the tunnel. Footsteps. Many footsteps. The sound of a patrol moving through the sewer with the purposeful rhythm of people who know exactly where they're going.

Jochen's flashlight went out. In the sudden, absolute darkness, Helena felt his hand find hers. She felt Marysia's arms tighten around her neck. She felt the water move around their legs, slow and dark and indifferent.

"Don't move," Jochen whispered. "Don't breathe."

They stood in the darkness. They listened. The footsteps grew louder—closer. The sweep of a flashlight beam appeared in the tunnel behind them—a German military flashlight, powerful, its beam cutting through the darkness like a blade.

They had perhaps thirty seconds before the light reached them.

Jochen let go of Helena's hand. She felt him move—felt him step away from her, into the water, toward the tunnel wall. She heard him wrench something free—a pipe, a bar, a piece of metal—and she understood, with the sudden clarity that comes in moments of extreme danger, what he was doing.

"Jochen—"

"Go. Take Marysia. There's a junction fifty meters ahead—take the left fork. It leads to the surface at—go, Helena. *Go*."

"No—"

"*Go.*"

His voice was not a plea. It was a command—the voice of an officer, the voice of a man who has made a decision and will not be dissuaded from it. Helena felt the weight of the decision in the air between them—a decision that was not new, that had been building since the day he saw her in the street, since the day he signed the release, since the day he chose her over his family and now, in a sewer under a burning city, was choosing her one final time.

She turned. She ran. She ran through the water with Marysia on her back, her boots splashing, her breath coming in ragged gasps, the darkness pressing against her like a physical thing. Behind her, she heard the German patrol's flashlight beam sweep the tunnel, heard a shouted command, heard the sound of impact—metal on metal, or metal on flesh, she couldn't tell—and then the sound of shouting and splashing and the chaos of violence in a confined space.

She did not look back.

She reached the junction. She took the left fork. She climbed the iron ladder to a manhole that opened onto a street in Praga—the Praga side of the river, where the Germans had not yet reached, where the city was still Polish, where the sky was the color of smoke but was, nonetheless, a sky that belonged to them.

She emerged onto the street. She set Marysia down. The girl was shaking but silent, her face grey with sewer-water and fear. Helena knelt in front of her and held her face in her hands.

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"Then walk. Walk with me. We're going to find a place to stay. We're going to—"

"Mama. Where's—"

Helena closed her eyes. She held Marysia's face. She pressed her forehead against her daughter's forehead. She breathed.

"He's brave," she said. "He's the bravest man I've ever known. And he—" She stopped. She couldn't say it. She couldn't say the word that meant *gone*, that meant *lost*, that meant *the thing you love most in the world has been taken from you and will not come back*. "He's gone, kochanie. He stayed so we could get out. He—"

Marysia began to cry. It was the first time she had cried since the roundup—the first time the dam had broken, the first time the nine-year-old girl had allowed herself the luxury of grief. She cried against her mother's shoulder, her small body convulsing with the force of it, and Helena held her and rocked her and wept with her, the two of them kneeling in the street in the dying city, crying for a man who had spent ten years running from love and ten years running back to it and who had, in the end, found it not in a lecture hall or a garden or a narrow bed, but in a sewer under a burning city, where he had stayed so that the woman he loved and the daughter he had never known could walk into the light.

---

Jochen did not die in the sewer.

She learned this three days later, from a resistance fighter who had been in the tunnel system and who had witnessed the aftermath. The patrol had been a German cleaning unit—soldiers tasked with clearing the sewers of Polish fighters who used them as escape routes. Jochen had fought them—a single man against five, armed with a pipe and a flashlight and the desperate strength of a man with something to protect. He had killed two of them. He had wounded another. The remaining two had shot him—three rounds, center mass, the efficient violence of trained soldiers dispatching a threat.

But he had not died immediately. He had lain in the sewer water, bleeding, and a Polish resistance fighter—someone who was moving through the tunnels on his own mission—had found him. The fighter had recognized the uniform under the civilian clothes. He had raised his weapon to finish the job.

And Jochen had spoken. In Polish. In the Polish he had learned from a woman he loved, in the language of the country he had helped occupy, in the tongue of the people he had served for four years as an officer of the occupying power. He had said: *Tell Helena Wojtera. On ulica Mokotowska. Tell her I lived long enough to choose correctly. Tell her—*

The fighter had not heard the rest. Jochen had died in the sewer, in the dark, in the water, with a Polish name on his lips and a Polish woman in his heart and the first truly uncowardly act of his life still echoing in the tunnel like a sound that refuses to stop.

The fighter found Helena in Praga. He told her what he had seen. He told her what Jochen had said. And Helena—Helena who had not wept since the courtyard, who had held herself rigid through the loss of her cell, the destruction of her city, the flight through the sewer—Helena broke. She broke in the way that a riverbank breaks when the water rises too high: not with a crash but with a slow, total collapse, the earth giving way, the structure dissolving, everything that had been held back pouring out in a flood of grief that was also, in its deepest and most hidden layer, relief.

He had chosen correctly. The words echoed in her mind as she walked through the ruined streets of Praga, holding Marysia's hand, moving through the landscape of a city that was being systematically destroyed around her. *He had chosen correctly.* Not his family. Not his name. Not his safety. Not the Reich. Not the uniform. He had chosen her. He had chosen the child. He had chosen the thing that was true over the thing that was easy, and he had done it at the end, in the last possible moment, when the choice mattered most.

It was not enough to undo the ten years. It was not enough to erase the war, or the occupation, or the child raised in secrecy, or the flat destroyed, or the friends taken, or the city burning. It was not enough to bring back the dead, or heal the wounded, or restore what had been lost. But it was enough to mean something. It was enough to be the final note in a symphony that had been dissonant for a decade—a resolution, not into happiness, but into truth.

---

They survived. She and Marysia survived. They survived the Uprising, the German retaliation, the winter of 1944, and the final liberation in January 1945 when the Soviet army entered the ruins of Warsaw and the survivors emerged from cellars and sewers and hiding places like creatures emerging from a long hibernation, blinking in the light, trying to remember what the world looked like when it wasn't on fire.

Warsaw was destroyed. Eighty-five percent of the city was rubble. The streets were unrecognizable. The buildings were skeletons. The Vistula still flowed, indifferent, carrying its dark water past the wreckage of a civilization that had been, five years earlier, one of the great capitals of Europe and was now a graveyard of stone and memory.

Helena and Marysia lived in a refugee camp on the outskirts of the city. They lived in a single room with twelve other people. They ate what the army gave them—thin soup, dark bread, the occasional piece of meat of uncertain origin. They waited. They survived. They breathed.

The years after the war were a different kind of war—a war against silence, against forgetting, against the temptation to let the past stay buried and simply move forward as though the earth had not been saturated with blood. Helena moved forward. She had no choice. The new government—the Soviet-backed government that replaced the occupation—demanded forward motion, demanded reconstruction, demanded the performance of optimism in a landscape of rubble. She worked. She found employment in a government office—translating documents, of all things, her German finally useful in a country that was now being administrated by a different set of occupiers who spoke a different language but maintained, in their own way, the same structures of control. She did not speak of the resistance. She did not speak of Jochen. She did not speak of the child who had been conceived in love and born in silence and raised in a city that had tried to destroy everything she held dear.

She spoke of nothing. She performed the role of the survivor—the competent, efficient, emotionally contained survivor who had lost everything and rebuilt from nothing and who did not require sympathy or understanding or the particular form of attention that the recently bereaved attract like flowers attract bees. She was, in the eyes of her colleagues, a woman of admirable resilience. She was, in the eyes of the few friends she maintained, a woman of impenetrable silence. She was, in her own eyes, a woman carrying an interior landscape so complicated, so contradictory, so laden with unresolved grief and impossible love that she could not begin to map it, much less share it.

Marysia grew. She grew in the post-war Warsaw of rationing and reconstruction, of new buildings rising from old rubble, of a city that was trying, with the desperate energy of the convalescent, to pretend it was healthy. She went to school—a real school, with a satchel and textbooks and a teacher who taught history and geography and the things that the occupation had tried to erase. She made friends. She played in the streets. She laughed, sometimes—a sound that was like music in Helena's ears, the sound of a childhood that was, finally, beginning to resemble something normal.

But Marysia was not normal. She could not be normal. The war had shaped her, as it shaped every child of her generation, in ways that were invisible to the casual observer but that would, over time, reveal themselves in the architecture of her personality—in her wariness, her self-reliance, her refusal to take safety for granted, her habit of counting the exits in every room she entered. She was a child of the occupation, and the occupation was in her bones, in her blood, in the particular quality of her attention that was always, always, scanning the environment for threats that might or might not be there.

Helena watched this and grieved. She grieved for the childhood her daughter had been denied, for the innocence that had been stolen, for the normal life that had been replaced by something harder and sharper and less forgiving. And she grieved, in the private chambers of her heart, for the man who had given Marysia those eyes—those dark, steady, assessing eyes that were both hers and his, and that carried in their depths the genetic memory of a love that the world had tried to destroy and had, in its typically clumsy and violent way, failed.

She thought of Jochen every day. Not in the sharp, agonizing way of the first months—the way of a wound still fresh, still bleeding, still demanding attention—but in the dull, persistent way of a scar. The scar was there. It would always be there. It ached sometimes—in certain light, in certain weather, in certain moments of unexpected beauty or unexpected loss—but it did not bleed. It was part of her. It was the landscape of her body, as permanent and as unremarkable as the shape of her hand or the color of her eyes.

Sometimes, in the small hours of the night, when Marysia was asleep and the flat was quiet and the city was quiet and the world was quiet, Helena allowed herself to remember. She remembered the seminar room. The library. The English Garden. The bridge. The flat on Amalienstrasse. The sex—the terrible, beautiful, consuming sex that had been the physical expression of an emotional truth too large for words. She remembered his hands, his mouth, the way he had held her, the way he had said her name. She remembered the morning after—the grey light, the cold flat, the feeling of being known in a way that was both terrifying and the most natural thing in the world.

She remembered these things and she did not weep. Weeping was for the early years. Now, in the later years, in the years of scar tissue and silence and the slow accumulation of a life that was, if not happy, then at least *lived*, she remembered with a kind of dry, clear-eyed tenderness that was its own form of love—not the passionate, desperate, world-ending love of the early days, but something quieter, something more durable, something that could survive the years the way a river survives the landscape: by flowing around the obstacles, by wearing down the stone, by continuing, always, to move forward.

And one day—a day in late March, when the first true warmth of spring touched the air and the sky was blue for the first time in what felt like years—Helena sat on the steps of the refugee center and watched Marysia playing in the courtyard with the other children. The girl was laughing. She was chasing a ball. She was nine years old, and she was alive, and she was free, and she was, for the first time in her young life, simply a child.

Helena watched her. She felt the sun on her face. She felt the warmth of the step beneath her. She felt, in her chest, the coal of memory—the memory of a seminar room in Munich, a garden in October, a bridge over a river, a narrow bed in a cold flat, a sewer in a burning city, a man's voice saying her name for the last time in the dark.

She took out a piece of paper—a scrap, a remnant, something she had found in the rubble of the flat on ulica Mokotowska, preserved by the accident of being under a fallen beam when the rest of the room was destroyed. It was a page from a book. A page from Heine. She had carried it with her through the Uprising, through the sewer, through the refugee camp. It was all she had left of the life she had lived before the war—not the Munich life, not the resistance life, but the *love* life, the life that had existed in the spaces between the other lives, the life that was hers and his and that no war could entirely destroy.

She read the page. The words were faded, the ink brown with age, but she could read them—she could read them because she had read them a thousand times, in a flat in Munich, in a library in Warsaw, in a cellar during a roundup, in a sewer under a dying city:

*In the lovely month of May,*
*when all the buds were springing,*
*then it was that love came into my heart.*

She folded the page. She put it back in her pocket. She watched Marysia playing in the courtyard. The sun was warm. The sky was blue. The Vistula flowed. And Helena Wojtera—the woman who had loved across the wreckage of the world, who had carried a child in silence, who had survived a war and a love and a loss that would have broken anyone with less iron in her soul—sat on the steps of a refugee center in the ruins of Warsaw and, for the first time in a very long time, allowed herself to hope.

Not for the future. She had learned, in the school of the occupation, that hope for the future was a luxury that the world did not generally grant. She hoped for nothing so specific, so vulnerable, so easily disappointed.

She hoped, simply, that the river would remember. That the Vistula, in its slow and ancient passage toward the sea, would carry with it the memory of the things that had happened on its banks—the love, the loss, the violence, the tenderness, the man who had chosen correctly in the end, the woman who had loved him despite everything, the child who had called him *papa* in a courtyard in a burning city. That the water would hold these things, the way water holds everything—gently, indifferently, permanently—and would carry them forward into a future that would, eventually, forget the war and remember only the river, and the people who had loved beside it, and the truth that love, even love across the wreckage of the world, is not nothing. Is never nothing. Is, in the end, the only thing that is.

She sat in the sun. She watched her daughter play. She breathed.

And the river flowed on.

## EPILOGUE

*Warsaw, 1965.*

Marysia was twenty-two years old. She was studying history at the University of Warsaw—the same university where her grandfather had taught, where her mother had once been a student before the war interrupted everything. She was tall, dark-haired, with the steady eyes of a woman who had seen too much as a child and had spent the intervening years learning how to look at the world without flinching.

She sat in the living room of the flat on ulica Mokotowska—the rebuilt flat, the new flat, the flat that had been reconstructed from the rubble and the memory of what had been there before. The wallpaper was new. The stove was new. The window looked out over a street that had been, twenty-two years ago, a battlefield and was now, once again, a street where people walked and children played and the ordinary business of living resumed its ancient, persistent course.

Helena was in the kitchen. She was fifty-seven years old. Her hair was grey. Her face was lined. Her hands—still strong, still precise—moved through the business of making tea with the automatic grace of long practice. She heard Marysia's footsteps in the hall and turned as her daughter appeared in the doorway.

"Mama. I found something."

"What did you find?"

Marysia held out a piece of paper. It was old—yellowed, brittle, the edges crumbling. It was a document. A German document. A military record.

"It was in the archives," Marysia said. "I was researching the occupation for my thesis. I was looking at the personnel records of the Wehrmacht administration in Warsaw, and I found—"

She handed the paper to Helena. Helena took it. She looked at it.

It was a discharge form. A military discharge, dated March 1944. The name: *Oberleutnant Jochen von Brandt*. The reason for discharge: *KIA. Warsaw Uprising. Sewer operations.*

And in the margin, in a different hand, a note: *Personal effects returned to—* The rest was illegible. Smudged. Lost.

Helena held the paper. She stared at it. The kitchen was very quiet. The tea kettle began to whistle.

"Mama?"

"I'm alright." Helena set the paper down on the table. She turned off the kettle. She poured the tea. Her hands were steady. Her face was composed. Inside, the coal of memory burned—brighter now, hotter, the accumulated heat of twenty-two years of silence finally finding, in this scrap of paper, a voice.

"He was your father," Helena said.

"I know."

"I never told you—"

"I know, Mama. I've always known."

Helena looked at her daughter. Marysia's eyes were steady. The eyes that were hers and his—the eyes that held in their dark depths the genetic argument of two people who had loved each other across the wreckage of the world and had, in the end, found their way back to each other, if not in life, then in the only country that outlasts life: the country of memory, of water, of a river that flows on and on and remembers everything the world tries to forget.

"What do we do with it?" Marysia asked.

Helena looked at the paper. She looked at her daughter. She looked out the window at the street—the rebuilt street, the living street, the street where children played and the Vistula flowed and the world continued its ancient business of destruction and renewal, destruction and renewal, without end.

"We keep it," she said. "We keep it the way the river keeps things. Gently. Indifferently. Permanently. We keep it because it's true. And because the truth—even the hard truth, even the truth that hurts—is all we have that the war couldn't take away."

She picked up the paper. She folded it carefully. She put it in her pocket, next to the other scrap—the page from Heine that she had carried for twenty-two years, the page that had survived the Uprising and the sewer and the refugee camp and the long slow reconstruction of a life from nothing.

Two scraps of paper. Two pieces of a story that the world would never fully know. Two fragments of a love that had been, against all odds and all reason, the most real thing in a world devoted to unreality.

She poured the tea. She sat with her daughter. They drank in silence. And outside, the Vistula flowed—dark, ancient, indifferent, carrying with it the memory of a city, a war, a love, and a man who had, in the end, chosen correctly.

The river remembered.

The river always remembers.
